Most adults have heard of the 150 minute target for weekly exercise. Fewer have heard what the guidelines say about doing more than that. This post explains the base target, what the federal guidelines say about going past it, and the cautions that come with higher amounts of activity. It is general education drawn from named public health sources.

## The base target

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity a week. Vigorous activity can stand in for part of that, at 75 minutes per week. The CDC also says adults need at least 2 days of muscle-strengthening activity each week, working all the major muscle groups.

That is the baseline. It is a target for health, and it is not a ceiling.

## What the CDC says about going beyond it

The CDC page states that exceeding 150 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, brings even more health benefits. In other words, the first 150 minutes are not the end of the benefit. More activity continues to add to it.

The guideline is built this way on purpose. A base target is something most adults can reach and keep up. The extra benefit gives people who want more a reason to add to it.

## The range in the full guidelines

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, published by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, give a wider range. They describe at least 150 minutes to 300 minutes a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity as the range for substantial health benefit.

The same document reports that people who are active about 150 minutes a week have a 33 percent lower risk of all-cause mortality than inactive people. It also says cardiovascular risk falls significantly at the 150 minute level, and that greater amounts reduce risk further.

The guidelines add that many people need more than 150 minutes a week to maintain their weight.

:::note Worth knowing
The 2nd edition of the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans says additional health benefits are gained by doing physical activity beyond the equivalent of 300 minutes a week of moderate activity.
:::

## Past 300 minutes

The guidelines say that people aiming to lose more than 5 percent of their body weight, or to keep off a large weight loss, may need to do more than 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week.

The document also gives its best estimate of the volume linked to the greatest reduction in risk, which is about 60 to 75 minutes per day of moderate activity. It notes that this high volume of moderate to vigorous activity is reached by a very small share of the population. Most people will not be near that level, and the guidelines do not present it as a requirement.

On safety at high volumes, the guidelines report no evidence of increased risk even at 3 to 5 times the key guidelines. That finding comes with the cautions covered in the next section.

## The cautions

The same guidelines are clear that more activity should be added with care.

First, increase gradually. The guidelines say to increase physical activity gradually over time to meet key guidelines or health goals, and that people who are inactive should start low and go slow.

Second, injury risk rises with volume. The document says the risk of musculoskeletal injury increases with the total amount of physical activity.

Third, avoid sudden jumps. It says the risk of a cardiac event does increase when a person suddenly becomes much more active than usual.

Fourth, choose suitable activities. People should pick activities appropriate for their current fitness level and health goals.

The guidelines also note that very high levels of activity may carry extra risks, such as a higher risk of hip and knee osteoarthritis in elite athletes. They advise people with chronic conditions or symptoms to seek medical guidance, and to use appropriate gear and sports equipment.

Even with those cautions, the guidelines conclude that the health benefits of physical activity far outweigh the risks of adverse events for almost everyone.

## What this means for a weekly routine

Reading these sources together, a few plain points come out. The 150 minute target is the base, and the guidelines show that more activity brings more benefit. Muscle strengthening on at least 2 days a week is part of the base target, not an extra. When activity goes up, it should go up gradually.

A practical way to think about it is to count your minutes for a normal week first. If you are under 150, the first goal is to reach it. If you are at or above it, the guidelines show that adding more can still help, as long as you add it slowly and choose activities that suit you.

Weekly structure helps. Many people find it easier to keep a routine when it has fixed points, such as the same days for strength work and a regular time for a longer session. A scheduled group session is one way to keep a fixed point in the week.

## Putting it together

The federal guidelines treat 150 minutes of moderate activity a week as the base, and they say that doing more brings added benefit, including past 300 minutes. They also say to build up gradually, to expect injury risk to rise with volume, and to be careful with sudden increases. For almost everyone, they conclude, the benefits outweigh the risks.

If you are thinking about changing how much you do, use the sources named here, start from where you are now, and add a little at a time.
