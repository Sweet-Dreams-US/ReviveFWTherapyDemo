Many adults think about exercise as a number: how many minutes this week, how many sessions. The national guidelines add a second idea. They say it also matters how the minutes are spread. This post explains what the U.S. guidelines and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) say about spreading aerobic activity through the week.

## The weekly target

The CDC states that adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week. Adults can meet the guideline instead with 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent mix of the two.

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition, from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, give a wider range for substantial health benefits. Adults should do 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, or 75 to 150 minutes a week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination. The guidelines add that additional benefits come from going beyond the equivalent of 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC says adults should also do muscle-strengthening activities on at least 2 days each week, targeting all major muscle groups.
:::

## What the guidelines say about spreading it out

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans state it directly: "Preferably, aerobic activity should be spread throughout the week." The same preference applies to pregnant and postpartum women and to adults with chronic conditions or disabilities.

The CDC gives an example of how the weekly total can be divided. Thirty minutes on each of 5 days works. This fits the idea of spreading activity across the week instead of placing it all in one or two days.

:::note Worth knowing
The guidelines state a preference for spreading aerobic activity through the week. The sources used for this post do not compare weekend-only activity with spread-out activity, so this post does not make that comparison.
:::

## Short blocks count

A common belief is that a workout only counts if it lasts a certain length. The guidelines changed on this point. The 2008 Guidelines required activity bouts of at least 10 minutes. The 2018 edition removes that requirement. The Advisory Committee concluded that bouts of any length contribute to the health benefits associated with the accumulated volume of physical activity.

The guidelines give the example that climbing a few flights of stairs counts. The CDC also says sessions can be broken into shorter blocks. For someone with a busy schedule, this means a walk at lunch and a walk after dinner can both add to the week.

## How the pieces fit together

Putting the sources together, the picture is simple. The weekly totals set how much aerobic activity adults should aim for. The preference for spreading it through the week describes how to place it. The removal of the 10-minute minimum means the pieces can be short.

A person who reaches 150 minutes can do it in several ways: 30 minutes on 5 days, or shorter blocks on more days. The guidelines support the plan that works for the person's week, with the stated preference for spreading it out.

## Reading the numbers in minutes

The CDC treats 75 minutes of vigorous activity as meeting the same guideline as 150 minutes of moderate activity, so one vigorous minute counts for about two moderate minutes in the weekly total. A person who mixes the two can add them on that basis.

A few examples show how flexible the total is. Thirty minutes on each of 5 days adds up to 150 minutes. Fifteen minutes on each of 10 occasions across the week also adds up to 150 minutes. Because bouts of any length count, a week can be built from many short blocks or a few longer ones. The guidelines' stated preference is that the minutes be spread through the week, not collected into a single day.

Muscle-strengthening is separate from the aerobic total. The CDC lists it as at least 2 days each week, and those days can fall on the same days as aerobic activity.

## What to remember

The national guidelines set weekly targets for adults and state a preference for spreading aerobic activity through the week. They also say that activity bouts of any length count, so short blocks add up. The CDC's example of 30 minutes on each of 5 days shows one way to meet the target.
