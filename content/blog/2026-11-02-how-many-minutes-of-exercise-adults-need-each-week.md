Most people have heard that adults should exercise "a few times a week," but that advice is vague. The United States has written guidelines that give actual numbers. They are short, they are public, and they are easy to use once you know what the terms mean.

This article walks through the weekly targets for adults, explains how to tell moderate effort from vigorous effort, and shows how to turn the numbers into a plan that fits a normal week. It is general education. It is not a plan for any one person.

## The weekly aerobic target

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, published by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, say adults should do at least 150 minutes (2 hours and 30 minutes) to 300 minutes (5 hours) a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity. The other option is 75 minutes (1 hour and 15 minutes) to 150 minutes (2 hours and 30 minutes) a week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) states the base version of this target in simpler terms: 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of the two.

Aerobic activity means movement that raises your heart rate and breathing for a sustained period. The CDC gives brisk walking as an example of moderate activity and jogging or running as an example of vigorous activity.

:::note The numbers in one line
Adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent mix of both.
:::

## Moderate and vigorous are not the same

The two levels count differently, so it helps to know which one you are doing. The CDC gives a simple rule. A person doing moderate-intensity aerobic activity can talk, but not sing, during the activity. A person doing vigorous-intensity activity cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath.

The CDC also describes an effort scale from 0 to 10. On that scale, moderate effort is a 5 or 6, and vigorous effort starts at a 7 or 8.

This matters because the vigorous target is half the size of the moderate target: 75 to 150 minutes, against 150 to 300 minutes. Harder effort earns credit faster.

## Mixing the two levels

You do not have to pick one level and stay there. The guidelines accept an equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous activity. Because the vigorous target is half the moderate target, a rough way to think about it is that each vigorous minute stands in for two moderate minutes.

For example, a person who does 60 minutes of brisk walking in a week and 45 minutes of running is counting 60 moderate minutes plus 45 vigorous minutes. Using the two-for-one idea, the 45 vigorous minutes count like 90 moderate minutes, for a total near 150 moderate-equivalent minutes. Treat that as an approximation to help you plan, not as an exact rule from the guidelines.

## Spread it through the week

The guidelines say that aerobic activity should preferably be spread throughout the week. The CDC adds that you can break activity into smaller chunks of time. You do not need one long session to count.

That means a week can be built many ways. Thirty minutes on five days adds up to 150 minutes. Ten-minute walks, done three times in a day, add up too. Pick the pattern you will keep doing, because a plan you follow is more useful than a larger plan you drop in the second week.

:::note Short bouts count
The CDC says you can spread your activity out during the week and break it up into smaller chunks of time.
:::

## Strength is a separate target

Aerobic minutes are only one part of the guidelines. Adults are also advised to do muscle-strengthening activities of moderate or greater intensity that involve all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week. The CDC phrases it as at least 2 days a week, and gives weight lifting as an example.

Strength days are counted on their own. Lifting weights does not replace the aerobic minutes, and a long run does not replace the strength days. A full week under the guidelines has both.

## Some activity is better than none

The guidelines open with a plain message: adults should move more and sit less throughout the day, and some physical activity is better than none. The CDC repeats the same idea. Adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate-intensity or vigorous-intensity activity gain some health benefits.

The CDC also says that going beyond 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week brings even more health benefits. The guidelines add that additional health benefits are gained by physical activity beyond the equivalent of 300 minutes a week. So the numbers are not a pass or fail line. They are a range, and more activity within a safe level for you tends to bring more benefit.

If you are far from these numbers today, you do not have to reach them in one week. Start with the amount you can do and add from there.

## Turning the numbers into a week

Here is one way to lay out a week that meets the base targets. It is only an example.

Choose how you will reach roughly 150 moderate minutes. That could be three 50-minute sessions, five 30-minute sessions, or a mix of shorter bouts. Then choose two days for muscle strengthening. Those can be the same days as aerobic work or separate days, since the guidelines do not require them to be apart.

Write the plan down before the week starts. Then track the minutes you actually complete, not the minutes you intended. A short weekly check, such as a note on a calendar, is enough.
