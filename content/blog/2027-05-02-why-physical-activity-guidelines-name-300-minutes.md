Most people who look up the physical activity guidelines find the number 150. Fewer notice that the same guidelines also name 300. If you have ever wondered why a guideline would list two numbers, or what the second one is for, this article explains it using the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

## The two numbers in the guidelines

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition, from the Department of Health and Human Services, say that for substantial health benefits adults should do at least 150 minutes (2 hours and 30 minutes) to 300 minutes (5 hours) a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity. The other options are 75 minutes (1 hour and 15 minutes) to 150 minutes (2 hours and 30 minutes) a week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous activity.

The CDC's adult activity overview states the lower end of that range as its baseline: adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, which can also be 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity or an equivalent combination.

So the 150 is where the range starts and the 300 is where the moderate range ends. The range is a set of targets, not a single pass mark.

:::note Worth knowing
The 2nd edition of the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans says "Some physical activity is better than none." Adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate-to-vigorous activity gain some health benefits.
:::

## Why the guidelines go beyond 150

The CDC says that if you go beyond 150 minutes a week of moderate-intensity activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity or an equivalent combination, you gain even more health benefits.

The Department of Health and Human Services goes one step further. Its guidelines say additional health benefits are gained by doing physical activity beyond the equivalent of 300 minutes, or 5 hours, of moderate-intensity activity a week.

The reason is what the guidelines call a dose-response relationship. For most health outcomes, additional benefits occur as the amount of physical activity increases. The amount can grow through higher intensity, greater frequency, longer duration, or a mix of the three.

That is why the guidelines do not stop at 150. The 150 minute level is the point where substantial benefits are described, and the guidelines say more activity can add to them.

## What 300 minutes looks like in a week

Three hundred minutes is five hours. Spread over five days it is 60 minutes a day, and spread over seven days it is a little under 43 minutes a day. The guidelines say aerobic activity should preferably be spread throughout the week, so thinking in daily blocks is closer to how the guidelines frame it than saving it all for a single day.

Those are simple divisions, not a prescription. The guidelines present 150 to 300 minutes as a range, and the right place in it depends on the person.

## How to tell moderate from vigorous

The minutes only count toward the targets at the right intensity, so it helps to know how the CDC describes the difference.

By energy use, the CDC says activity that burns 3 to 5.9 METs is moderate intensity, and activity at 6.0 METs or more is vigorous. One MET is the energy used while sitting quietly.

By effort, the CDC uses a scale from 0 to 10, where sitting is 0 and maximal effort is 10. Moderate intensity is a 5 or 6. Vigorous intensity begins at a 7 or 8.

The talk test is the simplest of the three. During moderate-intensity activity you can talk but not sing. During vigorous-intensity activity you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC describes the talk test this way: at moderate intensity you can talk, but not sing. At vigorous intensity you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath.
:::

The same activity can fall into different categories for different people, because the scale and the talk test measure how hard you are working, not what you are doing. A run done at a pace where you can still hold a conversation is a different effort from a run where you cannot.

## Strength work counts separately

The 150 to 300 minute range is about aerobic activity. The CDC adds that adults also need muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week. These should work all major muscle groups: the legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms.

Minutes of strength training do not replace aerobic minutes in the guidelines, and aerobic minutes do not replace strength work. They are two separate targets.

## Safety and the guidelines

The guidelines say to understand the risks yet be confident that physical activity can be safe for almost everyone, and that the benefits of physical activity generally outweigh the risk of adverse outcomes or injury.

A larger weekly target is not a reason to jump to it at once. How much a person should do depends on their own health, and that is a question for their doctor.

## The short version

The guidelines name two numbers because they describe a range. At least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, is the start. The guidelines also say additional health benefits come from going beyond the equivalent of 300 minutes of moderate activity a week. Use the talk test or the 0 to 10 scale to check your intensity, spread the minutes through the week, and add strength work on 2 or more days.
