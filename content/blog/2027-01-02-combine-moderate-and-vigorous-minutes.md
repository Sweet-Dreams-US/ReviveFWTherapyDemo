Most people do not spend a whole week doing one kind of exercise. You might walk on some days, run on others, and take a class once in a while. The federal physical activity guidelines were written with that in mind. They set one weekly target for aerobic activity and allow moderate and vigorous minutes to count toward it together. This article explains how the minutes add up.

## The weekly target

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) states that adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity a week. It gives 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week, as an example. The CDC also states that this can be 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous activity.

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, published by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, say the same thing in their key guidelines for adults. For substantial health benefits, adults should do at least 150 minutes to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, or the equivalent. The guidelines also say that aerobic activity should preferably be spread throughout the week.

## What counts as moderate and what counts as vigorous

Before you can combine minutes, you need to know which kind each activity is. The CDC gives two ways to tell.

The first is an effort scale from 0 to 10. The CDC states that moderate-intensity activity is a level of effort of 5 or 6, and vigorous-intensity activity begins at a 7 or 8.

The second is the talk test. The CDC states that as a rule of thumb, a person doing moderate-intensity aerobic activity can talk, but not sing, during the activity. A person doing vigorous-intensity activity cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath.

:::note Examples from the CDC
Moderate-intensity examples include walking briskly at 2.5 miles per hour or faster, recreational swimming, doubles tennis, and bicycling slower than 10 miles per hour on level terrain. Vigorous-intensity examples include jogging or running, swimming laps, singles tennis, jumping rope, and bicycling faster than 10 miles per hour.
:::

## The rule for combining minutes

The CDC page does not give a conversion number. The Physical Activity Guidelines do. In its section on active adults, the guidelines state that 2 minutes of moderate-intensity activity counts the same as 1 minute of vigorous-intensity activity. They also give an example: 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity is roughly the same as 15 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity.

That gives you a simple way to convert. To turn vigorous minutes into moderate-equivalent minutes, multiply by 2. To turn moderate minutes into vigorous-equivalent minutes, divide by 2.

The targets line up with this rule. The moderate target is 150 minutes. Half of 150 is 75, which is the vigorous target.

## Worked examples

Here are three weeks, each counted against the 150-minute moderate target.

Week one: three brisk walks of 30 minutes each, and two runs of 30 minutes each. The walks are 90 moderate minutes. The runs are 60 vigorous minutes, which count as 120 moderate-equivalent minutes. The total is 210 moderate-equivalent minutes, which is above 150.

Week two: 100 minutes of moderate activity and 25 minutes of vigorous activity. The 25 vigorous minutes count as 50 moderate-equivalent minutes. The total is 150, which meets the target exactly.

Week three: 90 minutes of moderate activity and 20 minutes of vigorous activity. The 20 vigorous minutes count as 40. The total is 130, which is 20 short of 150. In vigorous terms, the week is worth 65 of the 75 vigorous-equivalent minutes.

To check your own week, write down each session, mark it moderate or vigorous with the talk test, double the vigorous minutes, and add everything up.

## Short sessions count

An older version of the guidelines asked that activity happen in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The second edition removed that requirement. It states that bouts of any length contribute to the health benefits. It also says that adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity gain some health benefits, and that some physical activity is better than none.

That means a 12-minute walk at lunch can be added to your total. A short run before work can be added as well.

## Beyond 150 minutes

The guidelines state that additional health benefits are gained by doing more than the equivalent of 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week. The same minute-counting rule applies to that higher figure, so a person who does a mix of moderate and vigorous activity can use the same arithmetic.

## Muscle-strengthening is separate

The aerobic target is only one part of the guidelines. The CDC states that, in addition, adults need at least 2 days of muscle-strengthening activity each week, and that the activities should work all major muscle groups. Strengthening is a separate guideline from the aerobic target.

## A short summary

Adults are advised to get 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination. In the guidelines, 1 vigorous minute counts the same as 2 moderate minutes. Use the talk test or the 0 to 10 effort scale to decide which kind a session was. Short sessions count, and muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days is counted separately.
