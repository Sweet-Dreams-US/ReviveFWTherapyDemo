Most people who want to be more active run into the same question: how much is enough, and how fast should the weekly total grow? The national guidelines answer the first part with numbers. They also give a clear direction on the second part, which is to build up gradually. This article walks through what the guidelines say and how to think about adding minutes a little at a time.

## What the guidelines set as a target

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, published by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, set the main targets for adults. For substantial health benefits, adults should do at least 150 minutes to 300 minutes a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity. The other options are 75 minutes to 150 minutes a week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination of the two.

The guidelines also call for muscle-strengthening activity of moderate or greater intensity that involves all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists the same basics for adults: 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent mix, plus muscle strengthening on at least 2 days.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC says adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination, and at least 2 days a week of muscle-strengthening activity.
:::

## Going beyond the minimum

The 150 minutes is a minimum for the benefits listed, not a ceiling. The CDC says that going beyond 150 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, gives even more health benefits. The national guidelines frame the range as 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, so a person who reaches 150 has room to add more.

That is where small steps come in. A weekly total does not have to jump. It can rise by a few minutes at a time.

## Why small steps fit the guidelines

The guidelines say directly that activity should increase gradually. Their key guidelines for safe physical activity say to increase physical activity gradually over time to meet key guidelines or health goals. They also say that inactive people should "start low and go slow," starting with lower intensity activities and gradually increasing how often and how long activities are done.

The guidelines also say that aerobic activity should preferably be spread throughout the week. The CDC gives the same advice in different words: you can spread your activity out during the week and break it up into smaller chunks of time. Its example is 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week.

:::note Worth knowing
The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans say that inactive people should "start low and go slow," starting with lower intensity activities and gradually increasing how often and how long activities are done.
:::

## Short sessions count

An older rule held that activity only counted if it came in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The second edition of the guidelines removed that requirement. The guidelines also say that some physical activity is better than none, and that adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity gain some health benefits.

For planning, this means a weekly total can be built from pieces. A short walk, a few minutes on a bike, or a short session on a treadmill can each be one piece of the total.

## Knowing what moderate and vigorous mean

To count minutes, you need to know what intensity you are working at. The CDC describes a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 is sitting and 10 is maximal effort. Moderate activity is a 5 or 6 on that scale. Vigorous activity begins at a 7 or 8.

The CDC also gives a rule of thumb called the talk test. During moderate-intensity activity, a person can talk but not sing. During vigorous activity, a person cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath. Examples on the CDC page include walking briskly, at 2.5 miles per hour or faster, as moderate, and jogging or running as vigorous.

The distinction matters for the math. By the CDC's own targets, 75 minutes of vigorous activity stands in for 150 minutes of moderate activity. One minute of vigorous activity counts for about two minutes of moderate activity toward the weekly goal.

## A simple way to think about adding minutes

Here is the idea as arithmetic, not as a prescription for any one person. Suppose someone currently does 60 minutes of moderate activity in a week. The guidelines point toward 150. The gap is 90 minutes. If that person adds 10 minutes each week, they reach 150 in nine weeks. If they add 15 minutes, they reach it in six.

The right pace for a given person is their own to set. The guidelines say to build up gradually, and they say how often and how long can both grow over time. The size of each step is not set by the guidelines, so it makes sense to pick one you can repeat week after week.

## Putting it together

The national guidelines set a clear weekly target, make room for going beyond it, and tell inactive people to start low, go slow, and increase how often and how long they are active over time. Short sessions count, the effort level can be checked with the talk test, and the week can be spread across several days. Adding minutes in small, repeatable steps is consistent with all of it.
