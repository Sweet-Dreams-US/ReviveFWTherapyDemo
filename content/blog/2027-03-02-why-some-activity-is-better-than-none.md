Many people look at the weekly activity targets and decide they are too far away to bother with. If you are not moving much now, 150 minutes a week can sound like a different life. The guidelines themselves say something more encouraging. This post explains what they say about starting small and how to build from there.

## The target and the starting line are different things

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity a week. It also says adults need at least 2 days of muscle-strengthening activity each week. Those numbers are the goal for a full week of activity.

They are not the price of admission. The same CDC page states plainly: "Some physical activity is better than none." The target is where you are headed. The starting line is wherever you are today.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC says adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate or vigorous activity gain some health benefits.
:::

## Sit less, move more

The guideline message for people starting out has two parts. Move more, and sit less. You do not need to change everything at once. Standing up to take a call, walking to the end of the block after a meal or taking the stairs once a day all fit that message.

The CDC describes physical activity as supporting both physical and mental health, and says going beyond the baseline amounts brings even more benefit. That means there is no cliff you fall off if you miss the full target. Each step in the right direction counts toward something.

If you are new to regular activity, it also helps to measure progress by what you did instead of what you missed. Count the days you moved, not the minutes you fell short. A week with three short walks is a better week than one where you planned a big workout and did nothing. Over time the short walks tend to get easier, and that is the point at which adding minutes starts to feel reasonable.

## What moderate intensity feels like

You do not need a heart rate monitor to work out how hard you are going. The CDC offers the talk test as a rule of thumb. A person doing moderate-intensity aerobic activity can talk, but not sing, during the activity. A person doing vigorous-intensity activity cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath.

The CDC also describes effort on a scale of 0 to 10. Moderate effort is a 5 or 6. Vigorous effort begins at a 7 or 8. A second measure is energy use. Activity that burns 3 to 5.9 METs is moderate intensity, and activity that burns 6.0 METs or more is vigorous. You can ignore the METs and use the talk test or the 0 to 10 scale, which are easier to check while you are moving.

## Spread it out

The 150 minutes does not have to happen in one sitting. The CDC gives the example of 30 minutes on each of 5 days. If 30 minutes feels like a lot, shorter bouts across the day are a way to work toward the same weekly total. The point is to build a routine you can repeat.

A practical first week might be a few short walks, a bit more movement during the day and one session of something that uses your muscles. Then add a little more the following week. Small increases you can keep up are more useful than a big jump you drop after ten days.

## Add strength on 2 or more days

The muscle-strengthening part of the guideline is the one people skip most easily. The CDC says adults need at least 2 days of it each week. Strength work does not need to be long or complicated when you are starting out. What matters is doing it regularly.

## A simple way to think about it

Pick the smallest version of activity you would really do this week. Do it. Next week, make it a little bigger. The guidelines are built around the idea that more is better, and that some is better than none. You can start where you are.
