Fitness advice is full of words like moderate and vigorous. Public health guidelines use them too, and they matter, because the weekly targets change depending on which one you do. The trouble is that most people have no clear picture of what either level should feel like. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) gives a few simple ways to tell them apart, and none of them need a monitor or a lab.

## Why the two levels get separate targets

The CDC's adult activity guidance sets weekly targets for aerobic activity. It lists 150 minutes a week of moderate intensity activity, with brisk walking as an example. It lists 75 minutes a week of vigorous intensity activity, with jogging or running as examples. It also allows an equivalent combination of the two.

That means vigorous minutes count for more than moderate minutes. To use the targets well, you need to know which kind of effort you are doing when you do it. A walk that feels easy is not the same as one that has you breathing hard.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC lists 150 minutes a week of moderate intensity activity, or 75 minutes a week of vigorous intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of both, for adults.
:::

## The talk test

The simplest check is the talk test, and the CDC describes it in plain terms. During moderate intensity activity, you can talk, but not sing. During vigorous intensity activity, you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath.

You can use this during any activity. On a bike, on a treadmill, in a class or on the turf, notice whether you could hold a conversation. If you can chat easily and even sing a line, the effort is below moderate. If you can speak in full sentences but singing would be hard, you are likely at moderate. If you are down to a few words at a time, you are at vigorous.

## The 0 to 10 effort scale

The CDC also describes a relative effort scale. On this scale, 0 is the effort it takes to sit, and 10 is maximal effort. Relatively moderate intensity activity is a level of effort of 5 or 6. Relatively vigorous activity begins at 7 or 8.

This scale works because it asks about you, not about the activity. You rate how hard it feels to your own body right now. It gives you a number you can write in a training log and compare from week to week.

:::note Worth knowing
On the CDC's relative scale, where sitting is 0 and maximal effort is 10, moderate intensity is a 5 or 6 and vigorous intensity begins at 7 or 8.
:::

## Relative intensity and absolute intensity

The CDC separates two ways of measuring intensity. Absolute intensity is the amount of energy used during an activity, without considering a person's heart and lung fitness. It is measured in METs, and one MET is the energy used while sitting quietly. Activity that burns 3 to 5.9 METs is moderate, and activity at 6.0 METs or more is vigorous.

Relative intensity is the level of effort compared with the person doing it. This is why the same activity can land in different categories for different people. The CDC notes that an activity that is vigorous for a person of average fitness may be only moderate for someone who is very fit.

For everyday training, the relative measures are the ones you can use without equipment. The MET values are useful for comparing activities on paper, but they do not know how fit you are today.

## Using the tests across a week

Because both levels count toward the weekly targets, you do not need every session to look the same. A brisk walk that passes the talk test as moderate adds to the moderate total. A hard interval that leaves you speaking in short phrases adds to the vigorous total.

A few habits make this easier to track:

- Pick one method, either the talk test or the 0 to 10 scale, and use it the same way each time.
- Note the level next to each session, so the week adds up at a glance.
- Recheck now and then. As fitness changes, the same pace or the same weight can feel easier, and the rating changes with it.

The CDC also notes that some physical activity is better than none, so a short session at a lower level still counts for something.

## Muscle strengthening is a separate target

Aerobic intensity is only one part of the CDC's guidance. The same page says adults need muscle strengthening activity on at least 2 days each week, working all major muscle groups, including the legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms. The aerobic intensity tests do not apply to that target. It is counted in days, not minutes of breathing effort.
