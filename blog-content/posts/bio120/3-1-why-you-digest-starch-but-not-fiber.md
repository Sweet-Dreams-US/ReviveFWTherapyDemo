A slice of bread and a stalk of celery are, at the molecular level, both long chains of the same sugar. One of them gives you roughly four calories per gram. The other passes through you almost untouched.

The difference is not the plant or anything you can see on a label. It is the direction one bond points. That single detail explains fiber, lactose intolerance, why you can sprint, and why the joint supplement aisle exists.

## Glucose is a ring that closes two ways

Drawn flat, glucose is a six-carbon chain with an aldehyde group at one end and hydroxyls down the rest. Drop it in water and it does not stay flat. The chain curls around and the oxygen at the far end attacks the aldehyde, closing the molecule into a ring.

When it closes, the new -OH on that first carbon can point down or up. Those two versions are **anomers**, labeled alpha and beta, and in solution a single glucose opens and closes constantly, flipping between them — **mutarotation**.

By itself that is trivia. It stops being trivia the moment you start linking sugars together, because whichever way the bond points when the link forms, it is stuck.

## Same brick, different mortar

Two sugars join through a **glycosidic bond**, formed by removing a water molecule between them. Do it thousands of times and you get a polysaccharide.

Link glucose in the alpha orientation and you get **starch**, the storage form in plants. Link the exact same glucose in the beta orientation and you get **cellulose**, the structural fiber in celery, wood, and cotton.

The physical consequence is dramatic. Alpha chains coil into loose helices — open, floppy, easy for an enzyme to grab. Beta chains lie perfectly straight and stack into hydrogen-bonded sheets, which is why cellulose is strong enough to hold a tree up.

Your digestive enzymes — salivary and pancreatic amylase, plus the enzymes lining your small intestine — cut alpha bonds. You have no enzyme that cuts the beta bond in cellulose. Not a weak one. None.

Cows do not either. Their microbes do. And so do yours, partly, which is what happens in your colon: bacteria ferment some fiber into short-chain fatty acids that your gut lining uses. That is also why a sudden jump in fiber produces gas. You did not do anything wrong; you fed the bacteria faster than they were staffed for.

## The same logic explains lactose intolerance

Each disaccharide needs its own enzyme, because each is joined by a specifically shaped bond.

Sucrose is glucose plus fructose. Maltose is two glucoses. Lactose is glucose plus galactose, joined in a beta orientation, and the enzyme that cuts it is lactase.

Most humans reduce lactase production after early childhood. That is the global default, not a disorder — continued production into adulthood is the genetic variant. When lactose is not cut, it travels intact to the colon, bacteria ferment it, and gas and water follow. The mechanism is a bond angle and a missing pair of scissors.

## Why glycogen is branched, and why that lets you sprint

Your own storage form of glucose is **glycogen** — the same alpha-linked glucose as starch, with one modification. It is heavily branched, with a side chain sprouting off every dozen or so units.

Branching is not decorative. Enzymes chew glucose off the ends of a chain, so a molecule with hundreds of ends can be dismantled hundreds of places at once. That is the structural reason you can pull glucose out of muscle glycogen fast enough to fuel an all-out effort.

## Sugars are also structure, which is where the supplements come in

Carbohydrate is not only fuel. Long sugar chains called **glycosaminoglycans** — chondroitin sulfate, hyaluronan — hold enormous amounts of water, giving cartilage its compressive springiness and joint fluid its slipperiness. Attached to protein cores they form **proteoglycans**, the ground substance of connective tissue. Sugar chains hanging off cell surfaces, or **glycoproteins**, are how cells identify each other.

Which is exactly why glucosamine and chondroitin are sold for knees. The reasoning is honest on its face: these are real cartilage building blocks.

The honest limits are important, though. Swallowing a building block does not mean it arrives where you want it, and the larger, better-controlled trials on glucosamine and chondroitin for joint pain have been mixed at best, with the higher-quality studies generally the least impressive. That does not make them useless for everyone. It does mean the claims on the box are ahead of the evidence.

Joint pain that persists, swells, locks, or changes how you walk is a physician conversation, not a supplement aisle one.
