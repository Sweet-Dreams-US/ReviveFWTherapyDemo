On a sprint strip, the first effort feels almost easy and the fourth feels like a different sport. Your muscles did not get weaker in ninety seconds.

What changed is which chemical assembly line is supplying the energy. Your body runs two of them, and the fast one runs out of room almost immediately.

## Everything trades in the same currency

Metabolism splits into two directions. **Catabolism** breaks molecules down and releases energy. **Anabolism** builds them up and spends it. Both sides settle their accounts in the same token: ATP.

Pathways run as long chains of small enzymatic steps rather than one dramatic reaction, for two reasons. Small steps let the cell capture energy in packets it can actually use instead of losing it as heat, and every step is a place to install a valve.

Worth knowing: the standing amount of ATP in your body is tiny — enough for a few seconds of hard work. It is money in circulation, not money in the bank. Everything below is about how fast you can regenerate it.

## Glycolysis is fast and small

**Glycolysis** takes glucose apart in ten enzymatic steps, out in the cell fluid, with no oxygen required. It spends 2 ATP up front to prime the molecule, produces 4, and nets 2 — plus two loaded electron carriers called NADH.

Its main control valve is an enzyme called phosphofructokinase-1, which is suppressed when ATP is plentiful and released when it is not. The pathway senses your energy state and throttles itself.

When demand outruns your capacity to process pyruvate with oxygen, pyruvate takes the electrons off NADH and becomes **lactate**. That is not a failure. It is the recycling step that lets glycolysis keep running when the slow line is backed up.

Two corrections worth making plainly, because both are still repeated in gyms. Lactate is not a waste product — your heart, other muscle fibers, and your liver take it up and use it as fuel. And it is not what makes you sore two days later. The burn you feel during the effort travels with the acidity and metabolite buildup of hard work, and it clears long before delayed soreness even starts.

## The cycle that barely makes any ATP

If there is oxygen and time, pyruvate goes into the mitochondrion. An enzyme complex strips off a carbon as carbon dioxide and attaches what is left to coenzyme A, producing **acetyl-CoA**.

Acetyl-CoA joins a four-carbon molecule and enters the **citric acid cycle**, goes around, releases two more carbon dioxide molecules, and regenerates the starting compound so the next acetyl-CoA can enter.

The cycle produces almost no ATP directly, which surprises people. What it actually produces is loaded electron carriers — NADH and FADH2. Think of it less as a generator and more as a stripping station, pulling electrons off fuel and handing them to the next stage.

## The turbine

Those carriers deliver their electrons to a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The electrons fall from one complex to the next, downhill in energy, and end up on oxygen — which is the entire reason you breathe.

Each drop releases energy, and the complexes use it to pump protons across the membrane. That builds a gradient: more positive charge and more acidity on one side than the other. Stored energy, like water behind a dam.

Then **ATP synthase** opens the gate. Protons flow back through a molecular turbine that physically rotates, and the rotation drives the assembly of ATP from ADP and phosphate. This coupling of a proton gradient to ATP production is called chemiosmosis, and it is where the overwhelming majority of your ATP comes from.

It is also the reason aerobic fitness is not optional for lifters. This line only runs as fast as you can deliver oxygen to it, and delivery depends on your heart, your capillary density, and how much mitochondria you have built.

One honest limit: this pathway diagram appears on a lot of supplement labels under phrases like "mitochondrial support." Having a real pathway printed on the bottle is not evidence the capsule changes it, and for most of these products the human evidence is thin. What reliably increases mitochondrial capacity is repeated aerobic work, which nobody can sell you at a markup.
