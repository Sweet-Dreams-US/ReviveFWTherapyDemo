Magnesium oxide. Magnesium citrate. Magnesium glycinate. Magnesium malate. Magnesium threonate. One mineral, five prices, and a guy in the aisle who is very confident that the expensive one absorbs better.

He might be right. He might be repeating the label. The way to tell is to understand what that second word does, because it is not decoration — it is the same chemistry that decides where anything you swallow ends up.

## A functional group is the part that does something

Most of an organic molecule is inert scaffolding — carbon chains and rings that hold everything in position and do very little. The action comes from small clusters of atoms bolted onto that frame — **functional groups** — and chemists organize the entire field around them, because molecules carrying the same group behave in predictable ways.

The short list you will actually encounter:

An **alcohol** is an oxygen and hydrogen pair, written -OH. Sugars are covered in them, and so is glycerol, the backbone of every fat in your body. A **phenol** is the same -OH attached to an aromatic ring, which is the structure behind the polyphenols in coffee, tea, and olive oil.

A **thiol**, -SH, is the sulfur version — why cysteine, garlic, and onions smell the way they do. Two thiols can join into a disulfide bridge, the bond that holds hair in its shape and staples many proteins into their folded form.

An **amine**, -NH2, is basic and tends to grab a proton. A **carboxylic acid**, -COOH, is acidic and tends to give one up. Join an acid to an alcohol and you get an **ester**, which covers dietary fats and most of the molecules responsible for fruit flavors.

## At body pH, half of these are carrying a charge

Here is the part that changes how you read a label: amines and carboxylic acids do not stay neutral inside you.

At the pH of blood, around 7.4, a carboxylic acid has mostly handed off its proton and sits as a negative -COO⁻. An amine has mostly picked one up and sits as a positive -NH3⁺. This is why a free amino acid in your bloodstream carries a positive and a negative charge at once. Chemists predict this with **pKa**, a number that tells you the pH at which a group is half ionized.

Charge is the whole game for absorption. Charged molecules dissolve happily in water and struggle to cross the greasy interior of a membrane. Neutral, fatty molecules do the opposite. Your stomach is strongly acidic and your small intestine is close to neutral, so the same molecule can be charged in one place and neutral in another — a large part of why absorption happens where it happens.

## Back to the shelf

Magnesium citrate is not a different magnesium. It is magnesium paired with citrate, and that pairing is far more soluble than magnesium oxide, which is genuinely poorly soluble. Solubility is real and it does affect how much dissolves and gets absorbed. That part is not marketing.

What marketing leaves out is the comparison. More absorbable than what, at what dose, measured how, and does the difference change any outcome you would notice. Better absorption of a nutrient you already have enough of is a lab result, not a benefit.

Esters get used the same way, sometimes legitimately. Vitamin E is usually sold as tocopheryl acetate because the free form oxidizes on a shelf; the acetate group protects it and is cleaved off during digestion. That is a functional group doing real work.

Then there is the cautionary version. Creatine ethyl ester was marketed as an upgrade to plain creatine monohydrate on exactly this reasoning — add an ester, improve absorption. When it was tested, it did not outperform monohydrate, and it degrades readily rather than delivering more. Monohydrate remains the form with far and away the most research behind it, and it is the cheap one.

That is the pattern. Swapping a functional group produces a plausible chemistry story in thirty seconds. Plausible and demonstrated are different categories, and the industry is not required to show you the second before selling you the first.

## The one bond worth knowing by name

ATP, the molecule everything in fitness gets described in terms of, is a chain of three phosphate groups joined by **phosphoanhydride bonds**. Those phosphates all carry negative charge and repel each other, so the arrangement is strained. Break one off and you release usable energy.

Which explains why swallowing ATP is not the shortcut it sounds like. It is a charged molecule taken apart in digestion like anything else. Your cells build their own, continuously, from what you feed them.
