The word antioxidant is printed on green tea, dark chocolate, face cream, and at least one mattress pad. In none of those places does anyone say what is being oxidized, or why you would want to stop it.

Worth knowing before you spend another dollar on the word: oxidation is not damage by default. It is how you turn breakfast into the energy that gets you through a set of squats. Once you see that, the label reads differently.

## Oxidation just means losing electrons

Strip it down and **oxidation** is the loss of electrons, **reduction** the gain. They always happen together, which is why chemists call the pair a redox reaction. In organic molecules it usually shows up as gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen.

A candle burning, iron rusting, and your body metabolizing a bagel are the same category of event running at different speeds. The difference is control. Fire dumps all the energy at once as heat. You take the electrons off food one step at a time and capture most of that energy in a usable form.

## NAD and FAD are the delivery trucks

You have seen NAD in supplement marketing. Here is the actual job.

**NAD+** and **FAD** are electron carriers, built partly from vitamins — niacin becomes NAD, riboflavin becomes FAD. When an enzyme strips electrons off a food molecule, it hands them to one of these carriers, which becomes NADH or FADH2 and physically walks the electrons over to your mitochondria. There they get passed down a chain of proteins and, at the end, handed to oxygen.

That is why you breathe. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor at the bottom of the staircase. Without something to take the electrons, the whole line backs up.

This is also the honest-limits moment on NAD precursor supplements. Human trials do show they raise NAD-related markers in blood. Whether that translates into anything you would feel is far less settled, and the confidence of the advertising has run well ahead of the data.

## So where do free radicals come from

A **radical** is a molecule with an unpaired electron. Electrons prefer pairs, so a radical grabs one from whatever is nearest — which turns that neighbor into a radical. It becomes a chain reaction, and that is what makes them worth talking about.

Some leak out of your own metabolism. Moving electrons down the mitochondrial chain is not perfectly tidy; a fraction escape early and land on oxygen, producing **reactive oxygen species**. Hard training produces more of them. So do smoke, ultraviolet light, and pollution.

An **antioxidant**, then, is not a magic category. It is a molecule willing to donate an electron and stay reasonably stable afterward, usually because that unpaired electron spreads across a ring or a conjugated system instead of sitting on one atom. Vitamin C, vitamin E, and glutathione all work this way.

Your body also runs its own enzyme systems for this — superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase. That built-in machinery does most of the work, and regular training is one of the more reliable ways to upregulate it.

## Why loading up around training can backfire

Here is where the marketing and the science genuinely part ways.

Those reactive oxygen species produced during exercise are not just wear and tear. They are part of the signal that tells the muscle and the mitochondria to adapt. Research giving high doses of vitamin C and E around training has repeatedly found blunted adaptations compared with placebo — the supplemented group trained just as hard and got less out of it.

That does not mean antioxidants are bad. It means the intuitive model — oxidation is damage, more antioxidant is more protection — is wrong at the doses supplements deliver. Eating these compounds at food levels is a different situation from taking grams of an isolated one after a session.

If you want the general principle: the stress is the point. Removing the stress removes the reason your body changed.

## One small bonus about a word you have been using wrong

The same acid-base thinking explains a phrase you hear in every gym. **Lactic acid** barely exists in your body. Its pKa is far below the pH of blood and muscle, so almost as soon as it forms it gives up its proton and circulates as **lactate**, a negatively charged ion. Lactate is not a waste product either — your heart and slow-twitch muscle fibers burn it as fuel.

The burn you feel in a hard set is real. It is just not lactic acid corroding your leg.
