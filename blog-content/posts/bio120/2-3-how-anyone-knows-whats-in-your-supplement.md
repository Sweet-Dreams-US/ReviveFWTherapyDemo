The tub says five grams per scoop. How would you know?

The company that printed that label also decided what went in the tub. In the United States, dietary supplements are not reviewed for safety or effectiveness before they go on sale — that framework was set by a 1994 law called DSHEA, and it puts the burden on regulators to act after a problem, not before. The only thing standing between a claim and reality is analytical chemistry, and it is worth knowing what those tests can and cannot do.

## Three instruments, three different questions

**Infrared spectroscopy** shines infrared light through a sample. Chemical bonds vibrate like tiny springs, and each type absorbs at a characteristic frequency. A carbon-oxygen double bond shows up in a narrow, unmistakable window. An -OH group produces a broad hump. IR is fast and cheap and it answers one question: which functional groups are present. It is a fingerprint check, not an identification.

**Nuclear magnetic resonance** puts the sample in a powerful magnetic field and listens to how hydrogen nuclei respond. Each hydrogen resonates at a frequency that depends on its chemical neighborhood — that is the **chemical shift**. The size of each signal tells you how many hydrogens are in that position, and the way signals split into clusters tells you what sits next door. Put those three readings together and you can reconstruct a full structure from scratch. NMR answers what is it, in complete detail.

**Mass spectrometry** takes a different approach. It ionizes the molecule, accelerates it through a field, and sorts by mass-to-charge ratio. You get the molecular weight directly, plus a fragmentation pattern — the specific way that molecule shatters, which is reproducible enough to serve as an identity. Mass spec answers what is it and how much, and it is sensitive down to vanishingly small quantities.

## Which is exactly how drug testing works

Anti-doping labs chain these together. First **chromatography** separates a messy sample into its components — gas chromatography for volatile compounds, liquid chromatography for everything else. Then mass spec identifies each piece as it comes off the column.

The workhorse is tandem mass spectrometry, written LC-MS/MS. The instrument selects one ion, deliberately smashes it, and watches for specific fragments. That two-step confirmation is what lets a lab find a nanogram of a banned compound in urine containing thousands of other things.

Then there is the hardest problem in the field. Some banned substances are molecules your body already makes. Testosterone is testosterone; you cannot identify it as foreign by structure, because it is not foreign.

The solution is genuinely clever. **Isotope ratio mass spectrometry** measures the proportion of carbon-13 to carbon-12 in the specific steroid molecules recovered from a sample. Pharmaceutical steroids are synthesized from plant sterols, and those plants fix carbon with a slightly different isotope signature than the mix that ends up in your own tissues. The lab is not identifying a different molecule. It is reading where the carbon came from.

## What this means if you are not a tested athlete

The practical risk for most people is not fraud. It is contamination. Athletes have tested positive for substances that appeared nowhere on the label of a product they took, and in sport, strict liability means what is in your body is your responsibility regardless of how it got there.

The functional answer is third-party certification. Programs like **NSF Certified for Sport** and **Informed Sport** test production batches against banned-substance lists using exactly the instruments above, and a product carrying one of those marks has cleared a real, independent screen.

Now the honest limits, because they matter.

Certification tests batches, not necessarily the individual bottle in your hand. It certifies against a banned-substance list — it is not a statement that the product works, that the dose is sensible, or that you need it. "Lab tested" printed on a label with no named certifying body is unverifiable and costs nothing to print. And a Certificate of Analysis is only meaningful if you can see which lab issued it and whether the lot number matches what you bought.

Certification also does not solve the underlying question of whether the ingredient does anything. Those are two separate problems, and only one of them has been checked.

## The Revive Tip

If you are in a tested sport, or you work in a field with drug testing, treat every unsealed powder as a risk you did not need to take. Check for a named certification and keep the container.
