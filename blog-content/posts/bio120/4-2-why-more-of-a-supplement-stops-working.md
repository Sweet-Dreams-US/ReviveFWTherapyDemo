The label says one scoop. You take two, on the reasonable theory that if some is good, more is better. For most of what sits on a supplement shelf, that second scoop does close to nothing.

The reason is not absorption, and it is not that you bought a bad brand. Almost everything a nutrient does, it does by feeding machinery that already has a top speed.

## An enzyme does not push a reaction — it lowers the hill

Most of the chemistry your body depends on would happen eventually on its own. It would just take years. An enzyme makes it happen in milliseconds without changing where the reaction ends up.

It does this by grabbing the starting molecule in a pocket called the **active site**, then stabilizing the strained, awkward halfway structure the molecule has to pass through — the transition state. Lower that barrier and the reaction rate climbs enormously.

The old picture was a rigid lock and key. The better one is **induced fit**: enzyme and substrate both flex as they bind, closing around each other. That is also where specificity comes from — the pocket matches one molecule in shape, size, and charge and mostly ignores the rest.

## Every enzyme has a top speed

Put a tiny amount of substrate in front of an enzyme and the reaction rate rises steeply as you add more. Keep adding, and the curve flattens.

That plateau is **Vmax** — the maximum rate, reached when essentially every active site is occupied and the enzyme is working as fast as its chemistry allows. Extra substrate at that point simply queues.

The companion number is **Km**, the substrate concentration at which the enzyme is running at half its maximum. A low Km means the enzyme is already near flat-out at ordinary concentrations, so adding more gains you almost nothing. A high Km means the enzyme only picks up speed when the substrate is genuinely abundant. Biochemists once straightened that curve into a line — the **Lineweaver–Burk plot** — to read both numbers off a graph.

That is the whole answer to the opening question. Many of the transporters and enzymes that handle nutrients are already saturated at intakes you get from food. Past that point, more input does not buy more output.

## Four ways to jam an enzyme

Inhibition is worth knowing because it is how a large share of medicine works.

**Competitive** inhibitors look enough like the real substrate to sit in the active site. Flood the system with actual substrate and you outcompete them, so the top speed is unchanged but it takes more substrate to get there.

**Noncompetitive** inhibitors bind somewhere else and change the enzyme's shape, slowing every copy. More substrate does not rescue it. **Uncompetitive** inhibitors bind only after the substrate is already in place.

**Irreversible** inhibitors chemically modify the enzyme for good. Aspirin is the textbook case: it permanently acetylates cyclooxygenase, so the effect persists until the cell manufactures fresh enzyme.

The practical takeaway is that plenty of plant compounds and concentrated extracts inhibit enzymes too, including the liver enzymes that clear prescription drugs. If you take medication, what is in your supplement stack is a pharmacist or physician conversation, not a guess.

## Vitamins are mostly enzyme parts

Many enzymes cannot function alone. They need a **cofactor** — often a metal ion like zinc, magnesium, or iron — or a **coenzyme**, an organic helper molecule that carries something in or out of the reaction.

A striking number of those coenzymes are built from B vitamins. Niacin becomes NAD+, riboflavin becomes FAD, pantothenate becomes coenzyme A, vitamin B6 becomes pyridoxal phosphate. When a helper is permanently attached, it is called a prosthetic group — the heme in hemoglobin is the familiar one.

Here is where the label language gets slippery. "Supports energy metabolism" is technically true of every single B vitamin, and it tells you nothing about whether you personally need more of it. If you are not short of the vitamin, the enzyme already has its cofactor installed and the pathway is running at whatever speed the rest of your physiology allows. Adding surplus does not speed it up, and most of the water-soluble excess leaves in your urine.

Actual deficiency is real, and it is diagnosed with a blood test and a doctor, not from a symptom checklist that matches everyone who is tired. And the direction that carries real risk is the opposite one: several fat-soluble vitamins and minerals accumulate, and they have upper limits worth respecting.
