By four in the afternoon the sides of your neck and the tops of your shoulders feel like guitar strings. You blame the monitor height, the chair, the week you are having.

Some of that is true. But every breath you took today was produced by muscles, and if the right one was not doing the work, a group of small muscles in your neck was covering for it — all day, without a break.

## The muscle that is supposed to be doing the breathing

Your **diaphragm** is a dome of muscle separating your chest from your abdomen. It attaches around the lower ribs and sternum and reaches down onto the lumbar spine by two tendinous legs called crura.

When it contracts, the dome flattens. That increases the volume of the chest, pressure drops, and air moves in. Relaxing lets it dome back up and air moves out. In quiet breathing, that is nearly the whole event, with the **external intercostals** between the ribs helping to lift the cage and the internal intercostals contributing on forced exhalation.

The diaphragm has a second job. It forms the ceiling of the pressurized container you create when you brace before a heavy lift, which is why breath and bracing are the same conversation.

## When the dome under-works, your neck takes the shift

You also have **accessory muscles of respiration**, and they are supposed to be part-timers.

The **scalenes** run from your cervical vertebrae down to the first two ribs, so contracting them lifts the rib cage. **Sternocleidomastoid** runs from the sternum and clavicle up to the bone behind your ear and does something similar. Upper trapezius, levator scapulae, and pectoralis minor join in. During a hard sprint or a Hyrox interval, that is exactly what they are for.

The problem is duration, not effort. When shallow upper-chest breathing becomes the default — and stress reliably makes it the default — those part-timers work every minute of the day. Nobody would program a set that lasts nine hours.

Behind the base of your skull sits the **suboccipital group**, four small muscles per side that make fine adjustments to head position. They are unusually dense with sensory receptors, which means they are more about feedback than force, and they respond poorly to being held on all day.

Worth saying plainly: headaches, jaw pain, dizziness, or symptoms traveling into an arm are not neck-tightness problems to solve with a massage gun. Those go to a physician or physical therapist.

## Your core is a cylinder, not a six-pack

The abdominal wall has four layers per side. **Rectus abdominis** runs vertically down the front, crossed by tendinous bands that create the visible segments. **External oblique** fibers angle down and in, like sliding your hands into your front pockets. **Internal oblique** runs the opposite direction underneath. **Transversus abdominis** is deepest and runs horizontally, wrapping you like a belt.

The flat sheets of those three lateral muscles wrap the rectus in the **rectus sheath** and meet in the midline at the linea alba. Together with the diaphragm above and the pelvic floor below, they make a pressurized cylinder. That is what bracing manages.

Here is where the industry got ahead of the science. For years, isolated deep-core activation — drawing in the navel to fire transversus abdominis — was sold as the fix for back pain. When it was tested properly against ordinary progressive exercise, the specialized version did not prove superior. General strength training, done consistently, holds up about as well. If your back hurts, the useful first step is an assessment by a physical therapist, not a specific activation drill you read about.

## The muscles holding your spine together that you will never see

Along your back run the **erector spinae** — iliocostalis, longissimus, and spinalis — three long columns that extend the spine and, more often, control it as it bends.

Deeper sit the **transversospinalis** group: semispinalis, multifidus, and rotatores, shorter muscles crossing only a few segments at a time. Deeper still are the true segmental muscles connecting one vertebra to the next, and like the suboccipitals, they carry a heavy sensory load. They tell your nervous system where your spine is.

You do not train these directly. They get trained when you load your trunk and ask it to hold a position: carries, deadlifts, unilateral work, anything that makes stiffness a requirement rather than a suggestion.

Breathe so the lower hand and your side ribs move first and the top hand stays quiet, with a slow exhale about twice as long as the inhale. Your neck should be doing nothing.
