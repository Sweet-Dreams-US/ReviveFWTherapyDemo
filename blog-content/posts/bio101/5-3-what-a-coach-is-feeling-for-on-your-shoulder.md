A coach runs a thumb along the top of your shoulder before doing anything else, and it looks like stalling.

It is not. They are building a map. What happens next depends on knowing what is underneath — and more importantly, what to stay off.

## Finding your way by bone

Bone is the only honest landmark. Muscle changes shape with position and tension. Bone stays where it is.

At the shoulder the sequence runs outward and then back: sternoclavicular joint, out along the clavicle to the acromioclavicular joint, onto the flat shelf of the **acromion**, off its back edge onto the spine of the scapula, inward to the vertebral border, then down to the inferior angle. The **greater tubercle** of the humerus sits just below and lateral to the acromion, where the supraspinatus tendon attaches.

At the hip: find the iliac crest, follow it forward to the **anterior superior iliac spine**, then back to the posterior superior iliac spine, usually right under the dimples. The **greater trochanter** is the hard knob on the side, and the ischial tuberosity is the bone you sit on.

This is not trivia. Every cue about scapular position or a stacked pelvis is a statement about the relationship between two of these points.

## The places nobody should be working

Some regions have nerves and arteries lying near the surface with nothing over them. These are not deeper-tissue-work areas. They are areas to leave alone.

The **anterior triangle of the neck** holds the carotid artery, internal jugular vein and vagus nerve. No massage gun, no sustained pressure. The **axilla** exposes the brachial plexus and axillary artery with almost no cover. The **cubital fossa** at the front of the elbow carries the brachial artery and median nerve, and the ulnar nerve runs in a groove behind the medial epicondyle — the funny bone is a nerve on bone.

Below and behind the outside of the knee, the **common fibular nerve** wraps the fibula with nothing protecting it; compress it long enough and you get foot drop. The **femoral triangle** in the groin crease holds the femoral artery, vein and nerve, and the **popliteal fossa** behind the knee holds an artery, vein and two nerves.

The rule for the person on the table: sharp, electric, burning or radiating sensation means stop. That is neural tissue reporting, not a knot releasing. Numbness or weakness that persists afterward is a physician or physical therapist conversation, not something to sleep on.

## Your left side was never going to match your right

Variation is the rule, not the exception.

Vertebral counts differ, and transitional segments at the bottom of the lumbar spine are common enough to be unremarkable. Muscles vary too: plantaris is missing in a meaningful share of people, palmaris longus in the forearm is absent in many, and extra or fused heads turn up regularly. Nerve branching patterns differ, and so do the arteries feeding your hand.

Ordinary asymmetry is everywhere on top of that. Your dominant arm's bone is denser and its muscles bigger. Shoulder blades sit at slightly different heights. Leg length differences within a small range are common and mostly mean nothing.

Practically: a photograph of your standing posture is not a diagnosis, and an asymmetry that has been there twenty years and does not hurt is usually just you. **A change is more interesting than a difference** — new asymmetry, new pain, or a loss of function you did not have last month is worth a professional opinion. A shoulder blade that always sat lower on your throwing side probably is not.

## Where anatomy knowledge comes from, and what each version hides

**Cadaveric dissection** shows the real thing, variation included. But preservation stiffens tissue and changes its color, donors skew older, and reaching one layer means destroying the one above it.

**Plastinated specimens** are durable, clean and excellent for spatial relationships. The tradeoff is that the dissection decisions were already made for you — you cannot go looking.

**Virtual atlases and 3D apps** are repeatable, layer by layer, with no smell and no scheduling. But they are models, usually built from one dataset or an idealized composite, so they show a body with no variation and connective tissue that does not behave like the real thing.

**Medical imaging** is the only one showing a specific living person, moving.

Here is the honest limit. Every anatomy image you have seen is either one particular person or an average of people. None of them is you. Anyone who tells you exactly what is happening inside your shoulder from the outside is giving you a probability, not a fact — and the good ones say so.
