Nobody has ever hit a personal record with their subscapularis. It moves no meaningful weight and it will never show up in a mirror.

But when your press stalls at the same weight for two months, when your knee drifts inward on the last squat of a set, when your foot starts to feel sloppy in mile four along the river — the limit is rarely the big muscle. It is usually something small doing a control job it was not prepared for.

## The shoulder blade is a platform, the cuff is the guidance system

Your arm hangs off a bone that is held on by muscle. **Trapezius** works in three functional parts: upper fibers help rotate the shoulder blade upward, middle fibers pull it toward the spine, lower fibers pull it down and assist that upward rotation. **Rhomboids** retract it. **Serratus anterior** wraps from the ribs around to the inner border and both pulls the blade forward and holds it flat against the rib cage.

That upward rotation matters more than most people realize. To get your arm fully overhead, the shoulder blade has to rotate to keep the socket underneath the ball. When it does not, you run out of room.

Inside the joint, the **rotator cuff** — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis — does something different from what its name suggests. Its main job is not producing rotation. It is holding the head of the humerus centered in a shallow socket while the big muscles do the moving. Shoulder pain that hangs around for weeks belongs with a physical therapist, not with a heavier band.

## Two compartments and one very crowded tunnel

Your forearm divides into an **anterior compartment**, holding the muscles that flex your wrist and fingers, and a **posterior compartment**, holding the extensors. If grip is what ends your deadlift sets, that anterior compartment is the trainable answer, and straps are a reasonable tool for the sets where grip is not the point.

At the wrist sits the **carpal tunnel**. Its floor is a curved row of carpal bones and its roof is the flexor retinaculum, a thick band of connective tissue stretched across them. Running through it are nine flexor tendons and the median nerve. There is no spare room in there at all, which is why anything that adds volume inside produces symptoms in the hand.

That is anatomy, not a diagnosis. Hand numbness that wakes you at night is a physician conversation, and it is one worth having early rather than late.

## Your hip has a second layer

**Gluteus maximus** extends the hip. **Gluteus medius** and **minimus** abduct it, and their more important job is keeping your pelvis level every time you stand on one leg — which, in a run, is constantly.

Underneath them sits a group most people never hear about: the **deep external rotators**, including piriformis and the obturators. They are small, they sit close to the joint, and they orient the head of the femur in the socket. Functionally they are the hip's answer to the rotator cuff.

The big movers are familiar. The **quadriceps** are four muscles, one of which crosses both hip and knee. The **hamstrings** are three, all crossing both joints, which is part of why they get strained during sprinting. The **adductors** are five, and adductor magnus is a genuinely powerful hip extensor rather than just an inner-thigh muscle.

When a knee dives inward under load, the honest first suspect is frontal-plane hip control, not the knee.

## Your foot is not a block

The calf is the **triceps surae**: gastrocnemius, with two heads crossing the knee, over **soleus**, which crosses only the ankle. Soleus does most of the work in walking and steady running, and because it does not cross the knee, seated or bent-knee calf work is how you load it properly.

Around the shin, **tibialis anterior** lifts the foot and controls its lowering after heel contact, **tibialis posterior** supports the inner arch, and the **fibular group** on the outside stabilizes against rolling. Inside the foot itself are layers of small **plantar intrinsic** muscles that hold the arch actively rather than passively.

The honest note here is that foot training has outrun its evidence. Short-foot drills and minimalist shoes do appear to change some strength measures, but claims that they resolve heel pain, prevent injuries, or reshape an arch are not well supported. The research on arch supports is genuinely mixed. If your feet hurt, that question belongs to a physical therapist or podiatrist who can look at yours specifically.
