Ask ten people at a gym whether running will cost them muscle and you will get ten confident answers, most of them wrong in opposite directions. One camp thinks any cardio undoes a lifting session. The other thinks the whole concern is bro-science invented to justify skipping the treadmill.

The actual research says something more specific and more useful than either. But to get there, you need a clear picture of what your body is doing when you go hard — and what the number on your watch is really telling you.

## You have three engines, and they all run at once

Every contraction you make is paid for with ATP, and your body has three ways of producing it. The popular framing treats them like gears that switch over at set times. They do not switch. They all run continuously, and what changes is which one is carrying most of the load.

**The phosphagen system** uses creatine phosphate stored right inside the muscle to regenerate ATP almost instantly. Enormous power, tiny tank — it dominates for roughly the first ten seconds of an all-out effort. A heavy set of five, a sled push down the turf, a 30-yard sprint: this is that system. Refilling those stores takes minutes, not seconds, which is precisely why heavy strength work calls for two to five minutes of rest and why cutting rest short makes your top set feel like a different exercise.

**The glycolytic system** breaks down carbohydrate rapidly without needing oxygen and carries most of the load from roughly ten seconds out to about two minutes. This is the 400-meter, the 20-rep squat set, the third round of a Hyrox station. It is also where the burn lives.

And the burn is worth correcting, because the internet has this exactly backwards. Lactic acid is not what is hurting, and lactate is not a waste product. Lactate is a fuel — your heart, your slow-twitch fibers and your liver actively use it, and your body shuttles it around to do so. The burning sensation tracks with the accumulation of hydrogen ions and other metabolic byproducts as production outruns clearance. Lactate happens to rise at the same time, which is how it got blamed. It is a bystander with bad PR.

**The oxidative system** uses oxygen to produce ATP from carbohydrate and fat. Modest power, effectively unlimited duration. It dominates anything past a couple of minutes, it runs the entire time you are at rest, and — this is the part lifters miss — it is the system that restores you between hard efforts. Better aerobic fitness means faster recovery between heavy sets. Conditioning is not the opposite of strength training. It is partly what makes strength training possible.

## Three ways to train conditioning, and who each is for

**Steady state.** Continuous work at a conversational pace, 30 to 60 minutes. You should be able to speak in full sentences but not comfortably sing. This is the least exciting and most underrated tool in the building. It builds the aerobic base that lets you recover between sets, between intervals, and between training days. If you only do hard conditioning, you never build it.

**Intervals.** Repeated efforts of roughly two to five minutes at a pace that is hard but sustainable, with roughly equal rest. This is where your ability to hold a high output for a long time gets built. Rowing, the assault bike, and loops on the conditioning turf all work.

**High-intensity intervals.** Short, near-maximal efforts — 10 to 30 seconds — with rest long enough to actually repeat the quality. This is potent and time-efficient, and it is also the option people abuse. Genuinely hard intervals are a serious recovery cost. Two to three sessions a week is plenty for most people who also lift.

A workable weekly shape for most Fort Wayne adults who lift three or four times: two easy conversational sessions and one hard session. That is it. The most common conditioning mistake is not too little intensity, it is running every session at a medium-hard pace that is too hard to recover from and too easy to build anything.

## Why "220 minus your age" is a bad number

That formula is an average drawn from populations, and individual maximum heart rates scatter widely around it. Being ten beats off in either direction is completely ordinary, and larger misses are common. If you are one of the people whose true max is well above the estimate, every zone you have been prescribed is too easy. If you are below it, you are hunting a number you cannot reach and wondering what is wrong with you.

Heart rate reserve is a better framework if you are going to use heart rate at all. Instead of taking a percentage of your maximum, you take a percentage of the *range* between your resting heart rate and your maximum, then add your resting rate back. It accounts for the fact that two people with the same max can have very different resting rates and very different actual efforts at the same displayed number. It is still only as good as the max you plugged in.

Two other things distort the number in ways nobody warns you about. Heat and dehydration drive heart rate up at the same workload — a July run along the river in Fort Wayne humidity will read ten or more beats higher than the same effort in October, at no extra benefit. And wrist-based optical sensors are unreliable during lifting and grip-heavy work, where wrist flexion and muscle movement wreck the signal. If heart rate matters to your training, use a chest strap.

Honestly, for most people the talk test and a rating of perceived exertion get you 90 percent of the way there for free. Can you speak a full sentence? Easy day. Can you get out three or four words? Threshold work. Nothing? That was an interval.

## The interference effect, without the hysteria

Now the actual question. When you train endurance and strength in the same program — concurrent training — does one blunt the other?

Here is a fair reading of the evidence. Interference is real, but it is narrower than the gym mythology suggests.

**Hypertrophy is largely fine.** Adding endurance work does not appear to meaningfully compromise muscle growth for most people at realistic training volumes.

**Power and explosive strength are the vulnerable ones.** Rate of force development — how fast you can produce force — takes the biggest hit. If your goal is a vertical jump or a fast first step, high-volume endurance work is a genuine trade-off.

**Modality matters.** Running interferes more than cycling, largely because of the eccentric loading and muscle damage of foot strike. If you must do a lot of conditioning during a strength block, the bike and the rower are gentler on your legs.

**Volume and proximity matter most.** The strongest predictors of interference are how much endurance work you do and how close it sits to your lifting. A few hours of separation, or putting the conditioning after the lift rather than before, blunts most of the problem.

The mechanism gets oversold. You may have read that endurance training activates a cellular pathway called AMPK that shuts down the muscle-building mTOR pathway, so the two are chemically incompatible. That story came from early cell and animal work, and it is far too tidy. In trained humans, most of what looks like interference is much more mundane: you are tired, your legs are beaten up, and your squat session suffers because of it. Manage fatigue and most of the problem manages itself.

Practical rules: do not put hard intervals the day before or the morning of a heavy lower-body day. If you lift and condition in the same session, lift first when strength is the priority. And if you are a general-fitness adult training three to five hours a week, you are nowhere near the volumes where this becomes a real conflict. Do the cardio.
