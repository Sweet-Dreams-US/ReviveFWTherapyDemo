For about thirty years the advice was to stretch before you exercise. Then it flipped, and stretching first became the thing that made you weak and got you hurt. Now half the gym does ten minutes on a foam roller instead and is not entirely sure why.

All three positions contain a grain of something real and a lot of overstatement. Here is what the evidence actually supports about getting your body ready to train — and what to do in the fifteen minutes before you touch a bar.

## Flexible, mobile and stable are three different things

These words get used interchangeably, and they should not be.

**Flexibility** is how much range a joint has when something else moves it. Lie on your back and have a partner lift your straight leg toward your chest — how far it goes is hamstring flexibility. Passive.

**Mobility** is how much of that range you can reach and control under your own power. Lie on the same floor and lift your own leg. The gap between those two numbers is the interesting part. Plenty of people have range they cannot access, which is a strength and control problem wearing a flexibility costume. Stretching more will not fix it.

**Stability** is the ability to hold a joint where you want it while force tries to move it somewhere else. Not stiffness — control.

You have probably encountered the joint-by-joint model, which says the body alternates: ankles want mobility, knees want stability, hips want mobility, lower back wants stability, upper back wants mobility, shoulders want mobility. It is a genuinely useful map. When someone's squat is limited, checking ankles and hips before blaming knees is a smart first move.

But treat it as a heuristic, not anatomy. The lumbar spine does rotate and flex, and it is supposed to. Knees rotate a little. No joint is purely one thing, and the model was never meant to be read as a rule about which joints are allowed to move. Use it to generate hypotheses, then test them on the actual person.

## Does static stretching before lifting make you weaker?

Sort of, sometimes, less than you have been told.

Static stretching means taking a muscle to end range and holding. The research on acute effects is reasonably consistent: long holds — beyond roughly a minute per muscle — can produce a small, temporary reduction in force and power output. Short holds, in the range of 15 to 30 seconds, inside a complete warm-up that includes movement afterward, show effects small enough that they are unlikely to matter to your training.

So the practical rule is not "never stretch before lifting." It is: do not spend ten minutes holding long stretches and then walk straight to a max effort. If you need a static stretch to get into a position at all — a calf stretch to reach squat depth, a hip flexor stretch before overhead work — take it, keep it short, then do some movement afterward before you load it.

The other stretching methods have their places:

**Dynamic stretching** moves a joint through range repeatedly and actively — leg swings, walking knee hugs, arm circles, deep lunges with a rotation. It raises tissue temperature, rehearses the ranges you will use, and does not carry the force penalty. This is the default before training.

**Ballistic stretching** uses bouncing momentum to push past normal end range. It has a place for well-prepared athletes in specific sports and almost no place in a general fitness warm-up.

**PNF**, or proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation, alternates contracting a muscle against resistance with relaxing into a deeper stretch — the contract-relax pattern. It is among the most effective methods for gaining range, and it also produces an acute force decrement. Do it at the end of a session or on its own day, not before your heavy set.

Worth knowing for the long game: static stretching done consistently over weeks genuinely does increase range of motion. Some of that appears to be tissue change and a lot of it appears to be an increased tolerance for the stretch sensation. And strength training through a full range of motion increases flexibility on a comparable scale. If you squat deep and do Romanian deadlifts to full range, you are already stretching. That does not mean stretching is useless — it means you may need less dedicated stretching than you think.

## A warm-up that does something: RAMP

The old warm-up was five minutes on a bike and some toe touches. RAMP is a better structure, and it takes about the same time.

**R — Raise.** Elevate heart rate, body temperature and blood flow. Five minutes of easy work: bike, rower, an easy lap of the conditioning loop. Warmer muscle contracts and relaxes faster and is more compliant. This part is not optional, and in a Fort Wayne January when you have driven over in a cold car, it should be longer than in July.

**A — Activate.** Wake up the muscles you are about to depend on. Glute bridges before a squat day, band pull-aparts and scapular work before pressing, dead bugs before anything heavy overhead.

**M — Mobilize.** Move through the ranges the session actually demands. Squat day gets deep bodyweight squats and ankle rocking. Deadlift day gets hip hinges with a dowel. Do not mobilize joints you are not about to use.

**P — Potentiate.** Ramp into intensity. Progressive warm-up sets on your first exercise, and for power work, a few short sprints on the turf or a couple of low-effort jumps.

That last letter connects to a phenomenon called post-activation performance enhancement — the finding that a heavy or explosive effort can temporarily improve the performance that follows it. A heavy set of three, rested a few minutes, then a jump that goes a little higher than usual.

The effect is real, and it is also fussy. It depends on how heavy the primer was, how long you rested — usually several minutes — how trained you are, and whether the fatigue from the primer is masking the benefit. Trained lifters get more from it than beginners. It is a nice tool for a sprint or jump day. It is not a foundation to build your program on, and any product or protocol marketed on it is overselling.

## What foam rolling actually does

The standard explanation is that you are breaking up adhesions, releasing fascia, or smoothing out knots. That explanation does not hold up. The force required to meaningfully deform dense fascia is far beyond what a person's body weight on a foam cylinder applies. Whatever is happening, you are not restructuring connective tissue in ninety seconds.

What does show up when it is measured: short-lived improvements in range of motion, generally lasting minutes rather than hours, and — this is the useful part — without the temporary strength loss that long static stretching can cause. Also reasonably consistent: reduced *perceived* soreness in the days after hard training. Whether tissue is recovering faster is a separate and much less settled question. Feeling less sore is worth something on its own; just do not confuse it with being repaired.

So use it, and use it honestly. Foam rolling is a decent addition to the raise-and-mobilize part of a warm-up, especially if a tight-feeling area is keeping you out of a position. It is a fine comfort tool after a hard session. It is not a treatment, it does not fix why something got tight, and if a spot has hurt for weeks, rolling harder is not the answer — that is a conversation for a medical provider.

One related caution, since it comes up: the sauna is not a warm-up. Passive heat does raise tissue temperature, but it does nothing to rehearse the movement or prepare your nervous system, and long heat exposure before a heavy session leaves most people flat. Save it for after.

## The Revive Tip

Build one warm-up you can run without thinking and use it every training day. Five minutes on the conditioning loop to raise, five minutes of activation and mobility for the patterns you are about to load, then two or three progressive ramp-up sets. On lower-body or sprint days, finish with two easy accelerations on the 30-yard strip — the turf is right there, and it wakes your legs up better than anything you can do lying down. The whole thing takes twelve minutes and it is the highest-return twelve minutes in your session. [Come try it on the turf](https://revivefw.com/grand-opening).
