Ask two coaches the same question — why does the cold plunge help — and you can learn almost everything you need to know about both of them.

One says it flushes lactic acid and resets your nervous system. The other says cold constricts blood vessels and lowers tissue temperature, which seems to reduce how sore you feel, though whether it does much beyond that is genuinely unsettled. The second answer is longer, less satisfying, and far more useful, and the difference between them is not vocabulary. It is whether the person understands what they are saying.

You do not need a physiology degree to tell these apart. You need four signals.

## Real understanding keeps the mechanism and loses the jargon

There is a specific skill in this profession: explaining something technical in plain language without changing what it means. It is harder than either alternative, and it is the clearest evidence of actual comprehension.

Two failure modes sit on either side of it.

The first is **jargon with no follow-through**. "It upregulates mitochondrial biogenesis." "It activates the parasympathetic nervous system." "It reduces systemic inflammation." These may be technically defensible, but delivered without translation they are being used as credentials rather than explanations. A person who understands the idea can immediately follow it with a plain sentence. A person who read it in a caption cannot.

The second is **simplification that changes the meaning**. "Cold flushes the lactic acid out" is easy to understand and it is wrong — blood lactate clears within about an hour of exercise, long before the soreness you are treating even arrives. "Sauna detoxifies you" is easy to understand and meaningless, because no one can name the toxin or how it was measured. Simple is not the goal. Simple and accurate is the goal.

The version that passes both tests sounds like this: "Your sarcoplasmic reticulum — the calcium storage tank inside a muscle fiber — releases calcium to start a contraction. Hard eccentric work seems to disrupt that process temporarily, which is part of why you feel weak the next day." Real term, plain explanation, honest hedge on the part that is still being worked out.

Listen for that structure. Term, translation, limit.

## "I don't know" is a competence signal

The most reliable thing a coach can tell you is where the evidence stops.

Most questions in recovery science do not have clean answers. The research runs on small samples over short blocks, and it is frequently mixed. Somebody who has an equally confident answer for everything — sleep, supplements, cold dose, red light wavelengths, tendon rehab — is not better informed than the field. They just are not tracking which parts they are unsure about.

A good answer to a weak-evidence question has a particular shape. It tells you the direction, the rough size, the time frame, and how you would know it was working. "The research on this is mixed. If it helps you, the effect is probably small — better sleep and less soreness rather than more strength. Give it six weeks and track your morning soreness score, and if nothing moved, we drop it."

That sentence does four things at once: it sets expectations, it commits to a test, it names a timeline, and it makes room for the answer to be no. Compare it to "this will change everything for you," which commits to nothing and can never be wrong.

Also watch how they handle being wrong. In a field that revises itself constantly, a coach who cannot name something they used to believe and no longer do has either stopped reading or is not being straight with you.

## Look at what gets written down

Documentation is unglamorous and enormously revealing.

A facility that keeps session notes can spot that your knee complaints track with your running volume, that you sleep worse in weeks you train late, or that a modality you have been paying for has not corresponded to any change in three months. A facility that keeps nothing has to rely on memory, and memory is generous to whoever is doing the remembering.

Three documents are worth knowing about.

**Session notes** record what you did, what you reported, and anything unusual. Brief, factual, no speculation about diagnoses.

**Program summaries** state what you are working toward, what the current block is doing, and what markers are being watched. You should be able to ask for this and receive something coherent. If nobody can articulate the plan in writing, there may not be one.

**Referral letters** are the mark of a professional operation. When a coach sends you to a physical therapist or physician, a short written summary — what you have been doing, what you reported, what prompted the referral — makes that appointment dramatically more productive. Ask for one. The response tells you whether this is a normal practice or a novel request.

You are also entitled to ask what happens to those records. As covered elsewhere in this series, a gym generally is not covered by federal health privacy law, so how your information is stored and who can see it is a policy the business chooses. Ask about the policy.

## The new problem: confident writing nobody checked

Something changed in the last few years, and it affects how you should read fitness content, including content written by people you otherwise trust.

A large share of what circulates now is generated by AI tools, and the failure mode is specific. These systems produce fluent, confident, well-organized prose, and they will sometimes invent a citation that does not exist — a plausible-sounding study, real-sounding authors, a real journal, and no such paper. The writing quality of a claim has stopped being evidence of anything, because fluency is now free.

The habit that protects you takes thirty seconds. Copy the study title and search it. If it exists, you will find it in a database like PubMed with a DOI attached. If you only find the article that cited it, be suspicious. Then check the second thing: does the study say what the article says it says. Real citations get misrepresented at least as often as fake ones get invented.

The same standard applies to coaches. Using an AI tool to draft a program, summarize a paper, or write a member email is reasonable — it is a drafting aid. Passing along output nobody verified is not, and the tell is usually a suspiciously specific number attached to a claim that no one can source. A coach who says "I read this somewhere, let me find it before I tell you it's true" is behaving well.

## Where this test breaks down

None of these signals are proof, and it is worth saying so plainly. There is a real limit here: evaluating an expert usually requires some of the expertise you are trying to evaluate. Everything above is a proxy.

Credentials complicate it further. A strong certification is meaningful and it does not guarantee good coaching, and some excellent coaches came up through paths that never generated an impressive-looking set of letters. Plenty of people who explain things beautifully program poorly, and a few who explain things badly get results anyway.

So treat these as a filter rather than a verdict. What you are screening out is confident nonsense, which is the most common problem in this industry by a wide margin — not certifying excellence, which takes months of working with someone.

### Five questions worth asking

1. How does that work, in plain words?
2. How sure are we about that, and what is the evidence like?
3. How big an effect should I expect, and by when?
4. What would tell us it is not working?
5. Where do you send people when something is outside your scope?
