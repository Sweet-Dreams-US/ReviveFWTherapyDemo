Some ideas in fitness are wrong. More interesting are the ideas that were reasonable when someone first had them, got repeated for forty years, and quietly stopped matching the research while nobody was watching.

Four of those are worth your time, because all four change what you should actually do this week. None of them is a case of scientists being wrong and influencers being right. They are cases of an old finding outliving the context that produced it.

## Long stretches before you lift are working against you

The gym-class version goes: stretch first so you don't pull something. Two claims are buried in there, and they have separate track records.

On performance, the evidence is fairly consistent. Holding a static stretch for a long time — roughly a minute or more per muscle group — is associated with a short-term reduction in maximal force and power output. The effect is modest and it fades, but if you hold a deep hamstring stretch for ninety seconds and then walk over to squat heavy, you are not helping yourself. Brief static holds folded into a broader warm-up appear to matter much less, which is worth knowing before you throw out stretching entirely.

On injury prevention, the honest answer is that stretching before exercise has never shown a reliable protective effect in the research. What does hold up is a general warm-up: raising tissue temperature, moving through the ranges you are about to load, and starting with lighter sets of the actual movement.

What static stretching does well is improve range of motion over time. That is a real benefit, and it is worth training. Just put it somewhere other than the ten minutes before your heaviest set — after training, or in its own session.

**The practical version:** five to ten minutes of general warm-up, dynamic movement through the ranges you will use, then ramp-up sets. Long holds go at the end.

## Cold plunging right after lifting has a cost

This one is uncomfortable because the plunge is genuinely useful, and the nuance is about timing rather than value.

Cold water immersion after resistance training has been studied reasonably well, and controlled trials have found that plunging immediately after lifting reduces the acute anabolic signaling that follows a hard set — and, across training blocks of several weeks, tends to produce smaller strength and size gains than active recovery does. The cold is doing exactly what cold does, which is dampen the local inflammatory and signaling response. That response is part of the message telling the muscle to adapt.

Three pieces of context matter before you cancel your plunges.

First, this concern is specific to hypertrophy and strength adaptation. If your goal on a given day is to feel functional tomorrow — you have a competition, a tournament, back-to-back sessions, a Hyrox event — dampening the response is the point, and the trade is a reasonable one to make deliberately.

Second, the effect is about proximity. Putting hours between your last heavy set and the water, or plunging on conditioning and rest days instead, keeps most of the benefit without paying the adaptation tax.

Third, the picture for endurance training is less consistent than for lifting, and effects on mood, perceived recovery, and sleep are a separate question from muscle adaptation. Cold does more than one thing.

**The practical version:** if you are training to get bigger or stronger, keep cold away from your lifting sessions. Use it on off days, on conditioning days, or several hours later. If you are training to perform tomorrow, plunge whenever it helps you show up.

## Lactic acid is not why you hurt two days later

This myth has an actual origin, which makes it easier to dismantle.

Early twentieth-century physiology, working largely with isolated muscle preparations, connected lactate accumulation to muscle fatigue and to the concept of oxygen debt. That work was foundational and it earned its place in the history of the field. Somewhere in the decades of retelling, "lactate is associated with fatigue during exercise" became "lactic acid pools in your muscles and makes you sore two days later." That second sentence was never supported by anything.

The timeline alone kills it. Blood lactate returns to baseline within roughly an hour of finishing exercise. Delayed onset muscle soreness peaks somewhere in the 24-to-72-hour window, well after the lactate is long gone.

What actually produces that soreness is mechanical. It follows eccentric work — the lowering phase, downhill running, anything where the muscle lengthens under load — and involves microscopic disruption of the muscle and connective tissue plus the inflammatory response that follows. That is why your first day back on the sled leaves you wrecked and your tenth does not. It is also why the burn you feel *during* a set, which involves hydrogen ion accumulation and other metabolic changes, is a completely different phenomenon from the ache you feel on Thursday.

Lactate itself has been substantially rehabilitated. It gets shuttled between fibers and organs and used as fuel. It is not a waste product sitting in your legs waiting to be squeezed out.

**The practical version:** stop framing any modality as "flushing lactic acid." Massage, compression, and plunging may help how you feel. They are not clearing something that is still there.

## What "detox" and "FDA cleared" actually mean

Nothing in a recovery facility detoxifies you. Your liver and kidneys handle that continuously, and no sauna, wrap, or chamber improves on them. When a service claims to remove toxins, ask which toxin, measured how. There is never an answer, because the claim is a mood rather than a mechanism.

The regulatory language is more useful to learn, because it appears on legitimate equipment and most people misread it.

**FDA clearance** usually refers to a 510(k) pathway, in which a manufacturer demonstrates that a device is substantially equivalent to something already on the market. It is a statement about similarity, not about whether the device produces the outcome being advertised.

**FDA approval** is a higher bar — the premarket approval pathway, where a manufacturer must show evidence of safety and effectiveness for a specific intended use.

Those are very different things, and "FDA cleared" is often placed in marketing where a reader will hear "FDA approved." Worth noting alongside this: the FDA has publicly stated that it has not cleared or approved whole-body cryotherapy devices to treat any medical condition. Whole-body cryotherapy — the nitrogen vapor chamber, two or three minutes, no water — is also a different intervention from cold water immersion, which has a considerably better research base. They get marketed as interchangeable and they are not.

Supplements sit in yet another category. Under the 1994 Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, supplements do not have to be proven effective before they are sold. The manufacturer is responsible for safety, and the FDA acts mostly after problems appear.

## Where this still gets uncertain

Being honest cuts both directions. We do not have a settled answer on optimal cold dose — the temperature-and-duration combinations used in research vary widely, and nobody has mapped the curve properly. Contrast therapy, alternating hot and cold, has not clearly shown an advantage over either one alone. Infrared saunas are frequently marketed using research conducted on traditional saunas, and they are different exposures.

And essentially nobody has studied what happens when you stack four modalities in one session, which is what a modern recovery facility invites you to do. Each piece has its own literature. The combination has almost none.
