You mention that your shoulder has been catching for a few weeks. The trainer rotates your arm twice, nods, and says it's your rotator cuff — probably a small tear, here are three exercises.

That confidence should worry you. Not because trainers are unqualified, but because nobody can diagnose a shoulder from across a gym floor, and the person who thinks they can is the person least likely to send you somewhere useful. The most valuable thing a good coach knows is where their job ends.

## What a certification actually authorizes

Two words get used interchangeably and mean very different things.

**Certification** is a credential from a private organization saying you passed their exam and met their standard. In this field the well-regarded ones include the NSCA, ACSM, NASM, ACE, and ISSN for nutrition. A good certification means real study and a real test. It is not permission from a government to practice anything.

**Licensure** is a state granting legal authority to practice a defined profession, with a board that can take it away. Physicians, physical therapists, athletic trainers, registered dietitians, and in most states massage therapists are licensed. That license comes with a legally defined scope — things only they may do — and with accountability if they do it badly.

So the practical boundary looks like this. A certified coach can assess movement, design and progress your training, teach technique, coach general nutrition habits, and talk with you about sleep and stress. That is a large and genuinely useful territory.

A certified coach should not diagnose anything, prescribe or adjust medication, treat a diagnosed injury outside the direction of the clinician managing it, or provide individualized medical nutrition therapy for a disease. Those belong to licensed professionals, and the rules vary by state, particularly around nutrition counseling.

The tell is language. "Your hip is tight and here is how we'll load it differently" is coaching. "You have impingement" is a diagnosis. Same person, same session, one of them across the line.

Worth naming a third term that gets confused with both: **accreditation** applies to the organization, not the individual. It means an outside body has reviewed the certifying agency's program. It says something about the quality of the exam, and nothing about the person who passed it.

## The symptoms that end the conversation

Some things are not training problems. If any of the following show up, the next appointment is medical, not athletic.

- Chest pain, pressure, or tightness with exertion — or the same sensation radiating into the jaw, neck, or arm
- Fainting, near-fainting, or unexplained dizziness during or after effort
- Shortness of breath at rest or at a level of effort that never used to cause it
- New numbness, tingling, or weakness, especially down one limb or in a defined pattern
- Sudden severe headache, or a headache unlike any you have had before
- Pain that wakes you at night, or that is present at rest and unrelated to position
- Unexplained weight loss
- Calf swelling with warmth, redness, or tenderness in one leg
- Changes in bowel or bladder control alongside back pain
- Any joint that is hot, visibly swollen, and painful without a clear injury

None of that list is a diagnosis. It is a set of situations where the cost of guessing wrong is high enough that a physician should look.

The same logic applies before your first recovery session. Cold immersion and sauna both place real demand on the cardiovascular system. If you have known heart disease, poorly controlled blood pressure, a seizure disorder, are pregnant, or take medication that affects blood pressure or your ability to regulate temperature, get clearance first. A facility should ask. You should answer honestly even if you would rather not.

A coach who has a referral network — a physical therapist, an athletic trainer, a primary care physician, a registered dietitian they can point you to — is more useful than one with an opinion about everything.

## Consent, records, and what happens to what you tell them

**Informed consent** is not the waiver you sign on a tablet. It is a conversation in which you learn what the session involves, what could go wrong, what the alternatives are, and that you can stop at any point. For cold immersion in particular, "you can get out whenever you want" should be said out loud before you get in, not assumed.

**Documentation** matters more than it sounds. A facility that writes down what you did, what you reported, and what changed can notice a pattern six weeks later. One that keeps nothing is starting over every visit and cannot hand off anything useful when you see a clinician.

Now the honest part, because most people have this backwards. **HIPAA usually does not apply here.** That law covers health plans, most health care providers, and their business associates. A gym or recovery facility generally is not a covered entity, so the health information you write on an intake form is typically not protected by federal health privacy law.

What protects it instead is professional practice — how the facility stores records, who can open them, whether staff discuss members in front of other members, and what the business does with your data. Those are policy choices, not legal guarantees. It is entirely reasonable to ask who sees your intake form and where it is kept. The answer tells you a lot.

## Messaging that helps and messaging that harms

The last piece of scope is what gets said out loud, which is not usually treated as a safety issue and should be.

Weight-inclusive practice means organizing coaching around behavior and capability rather than body size. It is not a claim that weight is irrelevant to health. It is a recognition that weight-focused messaging has a poor track record for producing lasting change and a documented association with harm — disordered eating patterns, exercise avoidance, and people staying away from the places most likely to help them.

The practical version is fairly simple. Goals stated in behaviors and capacities — sleeping seven hours, three sessions a week, adding weight to a lift, running the Rivergreenway without stopping — outperform goals stated in appearance, because you control the behavior directly. Food framed as fuel and preference rather than virtue and punishment. No "earning" a meal. No before-and-after wall as the definition of success.

Cultural humility is the companion idea: recognizing that your assumptions about what someone eats, how they were taught to move, what their schedule permits, and what health means to them are assumptions. The alternative is asking.

And when a member is dealing with something that sounds like disordered eating, a gym is not the right treatment setting. That is a referral, made privately and without a lecture.

## What none of this guarantees

A clean scope of practice does not make a coach good. Plenty of professionals stay perfectly inside their lane and program badly. And a lot of the boundary in the real world is genuinely fuzzy — the line between "coaching around an injury your PT is managing" and "treating an injury" depends on the state, the situation, and the relationship between the two professionals.

What you can reasonably expect is that the person coaching you knows the boundary exists, names it when they reach it, and has someone to send you to. If you have never heard your coach say "that's outside what I do," it may be because it has never come up. It is more likely that they aren't looking for it.
