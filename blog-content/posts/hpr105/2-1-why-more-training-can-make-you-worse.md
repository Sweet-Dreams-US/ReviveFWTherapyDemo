Three good weeks, and then everything gets heavy. The same warm-up sets feel like working sets. You're sleeping worse, not better. Nothing in the program changed, so the obvious conclusion is that you've gone soft and need to push harder.

That conclusion is wrong often enough to be worth understanding. Stress does make you stronger — but only inside a range, and only when the recovery side of the equation keeps up. Here's the actual science underneath the slogan, including the part the fitness industry has been quietly misquoting for sixty years.

## The rat study behind every "stress makes you stronger" post

In 1936, Hans Selye published a short letter in *Nature* describing what happened when he exposed rats to a variety of harmful things — cold, injury, toxic compounds. What struck him was that wildly different insults produced a similar pattern of response. He called it the General Adaptation Syndrome, and he described three stages: **alarm** (the initial shock), **resistance** (the body mounts a defense and copes), and **exhaustion** (the defense fails).

That model has been photocopied into nearly every strength and conditioning textbook, usually with a training curve drawn on top of it.

Here's what's worth saying plainly: Selye was not studying exercise. He was studying rats exposed to noxious agents, and his central claim was that the response was *nonspecific* — that the pattern looked alike regardless of the stressor. Training adaptation is close to the opposite. Adaptations to training are famously specific. Heavy squats and long easy runs produce different tissue, different enzymes, different results. You cannot get one by doing the other.

So GAS is a useful metaphor for "there is a limit and you can exceed it." It is not a model of how training works, it does not tell you your correct volume, and it never claimed to. When a coach cites Selye to justify a specific program, they are borrowing authority the original research doesn't lend.

## Two ways to picture what a hard week does to you

The picture most people have seen is the **supercompensation curve** — a tidy line that dips after training, climbs back, overshoots baseline, then fades if you wait too long. It comes out of mid-century sports physiology, and as a teaching cartoon it's fine. As a description of your Tuesday, it's too clean. It implies a single peak on a single day that someone could time for you, and nobody can.

The more useful model is **fitness–fatigue**, which came out of impulse-response work by Eric Banister and colleagues in the 1970s. It says every training session produces *two* effects at once, not one. It builds a fitness effect — small in size, slow to fade. And it produces a fatigue effect — larger in size, faster to fade. What you can actually do on a given day is roughly the fitness minus the fatigue.

That single idea explains most of what confuses people:

- You feel worst in the middle of a good training block, because fatigue is large and fitness is accumulating quietly underneath it.
- You feel unexpectedly strong a week into a lighter phase, because fatigue drained away and the fitness stayed.
- Adding volume feels bad before it feels good, and quitting during that window is why people cycle through programs forever.

You are not measuring your fitness when you feel tired. You're measuring the sum, and the sum has two terms.

Both of these are models, by the way, and models are simplifications on purpose. Neither one gives you a number for your Thursday. What fitness–fatigue gives you is a better set of instincts: expect the dip, don't panic in it, and schedule the lighter week rather than waiting for your body to schedule it for you.

## There's a right dose, and it isn't the biggest one

**Hormesis** is the phenomenon where a low or moderate dose of a stressor produces a beneficial adaptive response while a large dose produces damage. The relationship is an inverted U: not enough does nothing, the right amount drives adaptation, too much drives you backward.

Everything in this building follows that curve. Cold water immersion: enough to be a genuine stimulus, and the plunge is useful. Too long, too often, too cold, and you're just spending recovery capacity on being cold. Heat: same shape. Training volume: same shape. Even sleep restriction has a curve, though you're always on the wrong side of that one.

The honest limitation is that nobody can hand you your personal peak. The curve is real; its location is individual, and it moves with your age, training history, stress, sleep, and what month it is. There's no formula, no wearable, and no coach who can calculate it in advance. What you can do is find it empirically — hold a dose steady for four weeks, watch what happens, adjust one variable.

Which leads to the most useful rule in the field: **the effective dose is the repeatable one.** A recovery protocol you do twice a week for a year beats a heroic one you abandon in March. Fort Wayne in February is a very good test of that principle.

## The bill you don't see: allostatic load

The last piece is the one that explains why a program that worked in October falls apart in January.

**Allostasis** — a term introduced by Sterling and Eyer — describes how your body maintains stability by *changing*, adjusting its set points to meet demand rather than holding one fixed state. **Allostatic load**, developed largely through Bruce McEwen's work, is the cumulative cost of running those adjustments over and over.

The practical version: your body keeps one account, not several. Training draws from it. So do a bad quarter at work, a sick kid, a late-night commute in the dark, poor sleep, and a diet that's running short on calories. Your legs do not know that the deadlifts were the planned stressor and the deadline wasn't.

This is why the correct response to a stressful month is often to reduce training rather than to add a recovery modality on top. The cold plunge is not a payment into the account. Sleep is. Food is. A lighter week is.

So when life load spikes, training load comes down. That's not softness, and it isn't a lack of discipline. It's arithmetic that people who train for a living do on purpose.

### Signs you've crossed to the wrong side of the curve

There's no test for this, but there is a pattern. Watch for several of these showing up together over a week or two:

- Warm-up weights feel disproportionately heavy while your top sets are unchanged
- Sleep gets worse rather than better after hard sessions
- Resting heart rate drifts up over several mornings
- Motivation drops off before performance does
- Small tweaks and niggles accumulate in places that were fine
- You're irritable in a way your family notices before you do

Every one of those has other explanations — illness, a bad week, a new medication — which is exactly why no single one is a verdict. Treat them as a cluster. The correct response is almost never a harder session or a longer plunge. It's three to seven days of reduced volume, more sleep, and more food, after which most people find the strength was there the whole time, buried under fatigue.

The people who never do this are not tougher. They just take the deload involuntarily, usually in the form of an injury or a month of not wanting to go.
