In Indiana — and in every other state — anyone can print "personal trainer," "coach," or "recovery specialist" on a business card and start taking money. There is no state board for it, no license to revoke, no legal barrier at all. Some of the best coaches in Fort Wayne have letters after their name. So do some of the worst.

That doesn't mean credentials are meaningless. It means you have to know what each one actually promises, because the industry uses three very different words as though they were interchangeable.

## Three words that are not synonyms

**Licensure** is a state government granting you legal permission to practice a profession, along with the power to take that permission away. Physicians, physical therapists, athletic trainers, dietitians, and massage therapists all operate under some form of state regulation, with requirements that vary by state. Practicing one of those without a license is a legal problem, not a professional embarrassment.

**Certification** is a private organization saying you passed its exam and kept up its continuing education. It carries no legal authority. The organization can pull your certification; the state has no opinion. Personal training, strength coaching, and every recovery-adjacent title live here.

**Accreditation** is a third party evaluating the organization rather than the person. In this field the name to know is the NCCA — the National Commission for Certifying Agencies — which reviews whether a certification exam is built and scored to professional standards. Academic programs have their own accreditors. When someone says a certification is "accredited," ask by whom, because the honest answer is sometimes "by a company we created."

Put simply: accreditation vets the test, certification vets the person, licensure grants the legal right. Most gym-floor arguments about credentials are really confusion between those three.

## What the letters after a name actually authorize

Here are the big ones and what they signal.

**NSCA** — the National Strength and Conditioning Association. Its flagship credential, the CSCS, is built for coaching athletes and requires a degree. It signals someone trained in program design, periodization, and the weight room specifically.

**ACSM** — the American College of Sports Medicine. The most clinical of the group. ACSM writes the position stands and exercise-testing guidelines that much of the industry treats as its reference standard, and its clinical exercise credentials point toward working with people who have chronic conditions, under medical supervision.

**NASM** and **ACE** — the two largest general-population certifications, both widely held, both NCCA-accredited. They signal broad competence with healthy adults.

**ISSN** — the International Society of Sports Nutrition, which certifies in sports nutrition and publishes position stands on things like protein intake and supplement timing.

Two things that get confused with certifications are worth separating out. A **degree** in exercise science or kinesiology is education, not a credential — it means four years of physiology, biomechanics, and research methods, and it does not by itself authorize anything. Most serious coaches hold both a degree and a certification, and they do different jobs.

And **recovery-specific credentials** are their own category. Many of them come from equipment manufacturers or private academies, few carry independent accreditation, and some can be completed in a weekend. That doesn't make the training worthless — someone should know how to run a plunge safely and how to spot a person who shouldn't be in one. It does mean the certificate on the wall is telling you about equipment operation, not about physiology.

Now the part nobody says out loud. What all of those authorize is *employment*. A certification qualifies you to be hired and insured. It does not authorize you to diagnose anything, treat an injury, prescribe a diet for a medical condition, adjust a joint, or tell you to stop taking a medication. A credential is permission to coach, not permission to practice medicine.

## The honest limitation nobody in this business likes

Here's what we can't tell you: there is essentially no good evidence that any particular certification produces better client outcomes than any other. Nobody has run that study in a way that would answer it. What we have are exams of varying rigor, prerequisites of varying seriousness, and continuing education requirements that a motivated person can satisfy without learning much.

So treat a credential as a floor, not a ceiling. It tells you someone cared enough to study and can be held to a standard by somebody. It does not tell you whether they can watch you squat and see the thing that matters, or whether they'll say "I don't know" when they don't know.

The two best predictors of a good coach are unglamorous. First, they ask more questions than they answer in the first twenty minutes. Second, they refer out — readily, by name, without ego.

## When you need a licensed clinician, not a coach

This is the boundary that actually protects you. A well-trained coach will send you elsewhere the moment any of the following show up:

- Pain that wakes you at night, or pain that doesn't change at all with position or movement
- Numbness, tingling, or pain that radiates down a limb
- Chest pressure, unusual shortness of breath, or dizziness with exertion
- A joint that locks, gives way, or swells without an obvious cause
- Unexplained weight loss, or any new symptom you can't account for
- A diagnosed medical condition where you want the nutrition plan to match it

Those are physician, physical therapist, or registered dietitian conversations. Not because the gym is being cautious for legal reasons, but because a coach genuinely doesn't have the training or the legal standing to sort those out, and guessing wastes months of your life.

The flip side: a licensed clinician isn't automatically the right person for building strength or conditioning either. Rehab and performance are different jobs. The best outcomes usually come from a coach and a clinician who talk to each other.

### Five questions worth asking before you take advice

1. **What certification do you hold, and who accredits it?** You're listening for a specific answer, not a defensive one.
2. **What's outside your scope?** A good coach can list their limits without being prompted twice.
3. **Who do you refer to?** Real names of real clinicians means they've built a referral network, which is the single clearest sign of a professional.
4. **How would you know if this program isn't working?** If there's no answer, there's no plan — just a workout.
5. **What's something you changed your mind about in the last two years?** This is the best question on the list. Anyone who has genuinely kept up has changed their mind about something, and anyone who hasn't will struggle to answer it.

## If you're reading this from the other side

Some of you aren't picking a trainer — you're thinking about becoming one. The field is wider than the gym floor. There's the performance track (private facilities, colleges, teams), the clinical track (cardiac and pulmonary rehab, hospital wellness, which generally requires a degree and clinical credentialing), corporate wellness, and facility operations, which is its own profession involving staffing, programming, safety, and business.

The general rule is that the clinical and licensed pathways demand a degree and a board exam, and the coaching pathways demand a certification plus real supervised hours. If you're deciding, work backward from the population you want to serve: athletes, general adults, or patients. That answer picks your credential for you.
