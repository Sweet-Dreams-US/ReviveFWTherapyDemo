Most gym emergencies are not dramatic. Someone sits down on a bench looking gray, or slurs a word and laughs it off, or gets quiet in the corner because they cannot quite catch their breath.

Four situations account for a large share of what happens in fitness settings, and all four share a feature: the outcome depends on how fast someone decides this is not normal. Being sure is not the standard. Acting is.

## Chest pain that is not just a hard set

**Acute coronary syndrome** is the umbrella term for a heart attack and its near relatives — blood flow through a coronary artery gets blocked, and heart muscle starts dying downstream. Time is muscle.

The classic presentation is pressure, tightness, or squeezing in the center of the chest, sometimes spreading to the jaw, neck, back, or an arm, with shortness of breath out of proportion to the effort, cold sweat, nausea, or a sense that something is badly wrong.

Plenty of presentations are not classic. Women, older adults, and people with diabetes more often report fatigue, back or jaw discomfort, nausea, or breathlessness without impressive chest pain. "It doesn't feel like a heart attack" is a bad reason to wait.

Call 911, and do not let anyone drive themselves. An ambulance starts care in the driveway and gets a hospital ready before you arrive.

You will read that aspirin helps. Chewed aspirin does have a role in some heart attacks, but it is wrong for some people, and that call belongs to the 911 dispatcher or a clinician, not to a bystander or a coach.

## Twenty seconds to check for a stroke

A stroke is a blocked or bleeding vessel in the brain, and options narrow sharply with every hour. The public screening tool is **BE-FAST**:

- **B**alance — sudden loss of coordination or a stumbling gait
- **E**yes — sudden vision loss or double vision
- **F**ace — one side droops when they smile
- **A**rms — one arm drifts down when both are raised
- **S**peech — slurred, garbled, or the wrong words coming out
- **T**ime — call 911 immediately

The detail people forget is the last known well time: the last moment the person was definitely normal. Treatment eligibility often depends on it, and you may be the only one who knows.

Sudden onset is the signature. Symptoms appearing over seconds are a 911 call, even if they fade.

## When breathing itself is the problem

**Asthma** is airway narrowing and inflammation. **Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction** is the same narrowing triggered by exertion, and it shows up more in cold, dry air — which in Fort Wayne means January outdoor running does it to people who are fine indoors in July.

Mild version: coughing, wheezing, chest tightness a few minutes into hard work, easing with rest. Someone who knows they have it usually has a rescue inhaler and a plan, and if they do, help them get to it. Handing someone else's inhaler around is not a thing you do.

The version that needs 911: they cannot speak a full sentence, the lips or fingertips look bluish, they are using neck and shoulder muscles to breathe, or the inhaler is not working. A quiet chest in someone struggling is worse than a loud wheeze, not better.

## Anaphylaxis moves faster than anything else here

A severe allergic reaction can go from a few hives to a closed airway in minutes. Watch for two systems at once — skin plus breathing, or skin plus gut — in someone who just ate, got stung, or took a new supplement or medication. Hives, swelling of the lips or tongue, throat tightness, wheezing, vomiting, a feeling of doom, sudden lightheadedness.

Call 911. If the person carries a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, help them get to it — it is theirs, and they should not have to hunt for it while their throat is closing. Call 911 even if the injector works and they seem fine afterward, because symptoms can return hours later in what is called a biphasic reaction.

## The honest limits

Recognition is genuinely hard, and none of these presentations are reliable. People with real heart attacks sometimes look fine. People who look terrible sometimes have a panic attack. You will occasionally call 911 for something that turns out to be nothing, and that is the correct error to make. Far more people are harmed by a delayed ambulance than by an unnecessary one.

The standing rule for this series applies here: an article is not a certification. Hands-on training through the American Heart Association or the American Red Cross turns recognition into a response you can execute.
