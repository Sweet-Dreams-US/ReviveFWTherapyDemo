Someone goes down in front of you. Within about ten seconds you have to answer one question: is this the kind of collapse where I call 911 and start pushing on a chest, or the kind where I keep them still and wait for them to come around.

Trained responders do not guess. They run a checklist, in a fixed order, that produces an action rather than a diagnosis.

## The first pass is four questions, not a theory

The **primary survey** is a rapid scan for the things that kill people in minutes. Airway, breathing, circulation, and level of consciousness — the letters change between organizations, but the logic does not.

Are they responsive. Tap the shoulder, ask loudly if they are okay. Is anything blocking the airway. Are they breathing normally — look at the chest for no more than about ten seconds. Is there obvious severe bleeding.

Nothing on that list requires you to know what is wrong. It only asks whether something is interfering with air, blood, or brain right now. If the answer is unresponsive and not breathing normally, the assessment is over — call 911, start compressions, get an AED. For anything you think might be life-threatening, calling 911 is always the first move, not the last resort.

## Agonal breathing is the trap

This is the one that fools people, and it is the reason bystanders sometimes stand and watch a treatable cardiac arrest.

In the first minutes after the heart stops, many people produce **agonal respirations** — occasional gasps, snorts, or slow irregular gulps of air. It looks and sounds like breathing. It is not. It is a brainstem reflex firing as oxygen drains out, and it means the heart is not pumping. Bystanders see the gasp, conclude the person is breathing, and wait. Dispatchers are specifically trained to ask about it because of how often it happens.

The rule is normal breathing: regular, quiet, rhythmic. Anything gasping, snoring, or spaced out in long irregular intervals in someone who will not wake up should be treated as cardiac arrest until proven otherwise.

## How the three collapses actually look

**Fainting**, or syncope, is a brief drop in blood flow to the brain. It is common in gyms and saunas — standing up fast after heat, after hard exertion, or after a long set held under tension. The person goes down, and then, usually within a minute, comes back confused but talking. Breathing normally is the distinguishing feature.

**A seizure** involves stiffening, jerking, or a blank unresponsive stare, often followed by a groggy period that can last many minutes. The response is to protect their head, move hard objects away, time it, and never put anything in their mouth. Call 911 if it is a first seizure, lasts more than five minutes, repeats, or happens in water.

**Cardiac arrest** is unresponsive and not breathing normally, and it does not resolve on its own. Someone who faints wakes up. Someone in arrest does not.

Starting compressions on someone who fainted will wake them up and embarrass you both. That trade is not close.

## Once they are awake, the questions change

If the person is conscious and stable, the useful work is history. Emergency curricula teach it as **SAMPLE**: signs and symptoms, allergies, medications, past medical history, last meal, and the events leading up to it.

That last one matters most in a gym. Did this happen at the end of a hard interval. In the sauna. Standing up from a bench. After skipping lunch. Has it happened before.

It is also a reason to carry your own information: a heart condition, seizure disorder, diabetes, or serious allergy should be findable without guessing.

## Moving people, and when not to

The default is not to move anyone unless leaving them creates danger. If someone is unresponsive but breathing normally, the **recovery position** — rolled onto their side — helps keep the airway clear, and it is taught in every certification course.

If there is any reason to suspect a head, neck, or back injury, keep them still, keep them talking, and wait for people with equipment.

## The honest limits

Field assessment is pattern recognition under pressure, and even paramedics get it wrong sometimes. You are not being asked to be right. You are being asked to act on the one pattern that cannot wait.

And to say it plainly: reading this does not make you competent to do it. Hands-on certification through the American Heart Association or the American Red Cross is where this becomes a skill. An article is orientation.
