Most gym injuries are unglamorous. A barbell scrapes a shin. Somebody catches an edge on the turf and rolls an ankle. Small events, where the difference between a good outcome and a bad one is usually decided in the first few minutes by whoever is standing there.

A handful are not small at all, and one of them regularly gets shrugged off by exactly the people who should not shrug it off.

## Severe bleeding: press hard, and do not peek

For bleeding that is spurting, soaking through fabric, or pooling, the response is **direct pressure** — firm, focused, continuous, with whatever clean material is available. Then keep it there.

The most common mistake is lifting the cloth every fifteen seconds to check. Every time you look, you break the clot that was forming. If blood soaks through, add material on top rather than removing what is already there.

Call 911 for anything you would describe as serious bleeding. You are not expected to estimate blood loss.

Tourniquets and hemostatic dressings are real tools for bleeding that pressure will not control, and the national Stop the Bleed program teaches both. They are also easy to use badly. That course is where you learn them, not a paragraph.

## The small stuff still deserves attention

Knurling scrapes, turf burns, and rope burns are shallow, filthy, and prone to infection because gym surfaces are gym surfaces. Clean the wound, cover it, change the dressing. Over the next couple of days watch for spreading redness, increasing pain, warmth, pus, or fever — those turn a scrape into a doctor's visit.

Minor burns from hot surfaces get cool running water and a loose covering. Skip ice, butter, and the rest of the folk remedies. Anything large, deep, or widely blistered is a clinician's problem. And two things people forget: puncture wounds look tiny and carry infection deep, and tetanus boosters expire.

## Sprain, strain, fracture, dislocation — and why you cannot tell

The terms are not interchangeable. A **sprain** is a ligament injury, ligaments being the tissue connecting bone to bone. A **strain** is a muscle or tendon injury. A **fracture** is a broken bone. A **dislocation** is a joint out of position.

Here is the honest part: you cannot reliably tell them apart on a gym floor, and neither can a coach. A bad ankle sprain and a small fracture can look identical. Deformity, an inability to bear any weight, numbness, or a pop with immediate loss of function all mean imaging.

Do not try to put a dislocated joint back in. If you are stabilizing something while waiting, support it in the position you found it and check that fingers or toes stay warm and pink.

You will notice nobody said RICE. The acronym is being steadily revised, and Gabe Mirkin, the physician who coined it in the 1970s, has publicly walked back the rest and ice portions. Current thinking leans toward protecting the area briefly, then loading progressively as tolerated.

## The one you never walk off

A **concussion** is a functional brain injury from a blow to the head or a force transmitted through the body. Two things get missed constantly. You do not have to lose consciousness — most concussions involve no blackout at all. And symptoms can be delayed by hours, so feeling fine right afterward proves nothing.

Watch for headache, fogginess, dizziness, nausea, sensitivity to light or noise, feeling slowed down, unusual irritability, or trouble remembering the event itself. Anyone who takes a real knock to the head and shows any of it comes out of activity immediately and does not return the same day. Not the same session, not the same evening.

Returning while still symptomatic is associated with longer recovery and, rarely, catastrophic outcomes. Clearance is a medical decision made by a clinician following a graded protocol — not by the athlete, the coach, or the person who feels fine now.

Worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizures, unequal pupils, weakness on one side, or increasing confusion after a head injury is a 911 call.

## The honest limits

Almost none of this is a diagnosis you can make in a gym, and pretending otherwise is how injuries get worse. The value of first aid is buying time — a smaller claim than the internet makes, and the true one.

And as with everything in this series: reading it does not certify you. First aid and CPR certification through the American Heart Association or the American Red Cross puts these skills in your hands, and Stop the Bleed covers the bleeding half in about ninety minutes.
