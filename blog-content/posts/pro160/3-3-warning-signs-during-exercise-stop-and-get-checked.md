Training is supposed to be uncomfortable. Burning quads, a heart rate you can hear, legs that do not want to do the last set — all normal, all the point.

A small number of symptoms are not that. People miss them because they arrive in the middle of an activity where feeling bad is expected. If you remember one thing: discomfort scales with effort, and symptoms that do not fit the effort deserve attention.

## The red flags that end a session

Stop and get evaluated for any of these, even if they pass.

**Chest pain, pressure, tightness, or heaviness** brought on by exertion, especially if it eases with rest. This gets rationalized as heartburn or a pulled muscle more than any other symptom on the list.

**Shortness of breath out of proportion to the work**, or breathlessness that is new for you at a workload you have handled before.

**Dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting** during or immediately after exertion. Fainting during exercise is not the same as fainting standing up too fast, and it is worth a cardiac workup.

**Palpitations** — a heart that races, pounds, or flutters irregularly, particularly with lightheadedness.

**Unusual fatigue** you cannot explain, where a workload that used to be routine feels impossible for weeks.

**New swelling in the legs or ankles**, especially on one side, with pain or warmth.

**Any new neurological symptom** — one-sided weakness, numbness, slurred speech, vision change, or the worst headache of your life. That one is 911, immediately.

For any symptom that could be life-threatening, call 911 first. You are not obligated to be certain.

## Flags that come up before you start

Some things mean a conversation with a physician before your first session, not during it. A new diagnosis of a cardiac, pulmonary, or metabolic condition. A new medication that affects heart rate or blood pressure — beta blockers change what your heart rate data means. Recent surgery. Pregnancy, particularly for heat and cold services.

None of these are a no. Most are a "not until someone qualified has weighed in on the details."

## Who a good coach should have on speed dial

A professional who understands their limits finds referring easy, because they already know where to send you.

A functional referral network usually includes primary care physicians, physical therapists, a **registered dietitian** — the credential matters, because "nutritionist" is not a protected title in most places while RD is — and mental health providers.

A good referral is private, specific, and carries no diagnosis attached. "Here is a physical therapist I trust, and here is what I noticed" is a referral. "You have a rotator cuff tear" is a coach practicing medicine, and it is both illegal and frequently wrong.

## The ethics part nobody advertises

Working with people around their bodies is an unusually intimate professional relationship, and the standards reflect that.

Boundaries: the power imbalance is real even when it does not feel like it, and dual relationships — coach and business partner, coach and romantic interest — compromise judgment.

Dignity: no unsolicited commentary on someone's body, weight, or eating. No discussion of one member with another. Consent before hands-on cueing or spotting, every time, and before any photo or video. What someone puts on an intake form stays between the people who need it.

## Why a CPR card has an expiration date

Basic Life Support certification is not permanent. Both the American Heart Association and the American Red Cross issue credentials that expire, typically after two years, and renewal means getting back on the floor with a mannequin. Reputable fitness certifications require current CPR and AED certification to stay in good standing, plus continuing education on a cycle.

The reason is unglamorous: this is a perishable skill. Compression rate and depth decay quickly without practice, and the sequence you were confident about eighteen months ago will not surface cleanly under adrenaline.

Which is the right place to repeat what this whole series has been saying. **Reading an article does not certify anyone to do any of this.** Certification is hands-on, a few hours, and available locally through the American Heart Association and the American Red Cross.

## The honest limits

Red flag lists are blunt instruments. They generate false alarms, and most people who stop and get checked will be told they are fine. That is the cost of a tool tuned to catch the rare serious thing, and it is worth paying.

They also miss things. Plenty of cardiac events happen without a warning symptom anybody noticed. A clean checklist is not a guarantee, and no coach, form, or facility can promise you one.
