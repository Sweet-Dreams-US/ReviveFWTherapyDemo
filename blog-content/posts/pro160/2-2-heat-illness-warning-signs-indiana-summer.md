Fort Wayne in late July is not the hottest place in the country, but the humidity does something specific and nasty. Your body cools itself almost entirely by evaporating sweat. When the air is saturated, that sweat runs off you instead, and you lose fluid without getting the cooling you paid for.

That is the mechanism behind exertional heat illness. It is not about being tough. It is physics.

## The spectrum, and the one line that matters

**Heat cramps** — painful muscle spasms during or after hard work in the heat, usually with heavy sweating.

**Heat exhaustion** — the body is losing the cooling battle. Heavy sweating, dizziness, nausea, headache, weakness, clammy skin, a fast pulse, and a strong urge to stop. The person is uncomfortable and knows exactly where they are.

**Exertional heat stroke** — a true emergency. Core temperature has climbed high enough that the central nervous system is affected.

The dividing line between the last two is not how hot the skin feels or how much someone is sweating. It is **mental status**. Confusion, aggression, slurred speech, bizarre behavior, stumbling, or loss of consciousness in someone working in the heat is heat stroke until proven otherwise.

The skin can be wet or dry. "Hot and dry skin" is a widely repeated rule that does not hold in exertional cases, where people are often still drenched. Do not let the sweat talk you out of it.

Call 911. Get them out of the sun and into air conditioning or shade, and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

## Why the sports medicine version looks different

In athletic settings the standard of care is to cool first and transport second, because how fast core temperature comes down drives the outcome. The tool is usually cold water immersion, guided by a rectal temperature — oral, ear, forehead, and armpit readings are not accurate in an exercising person and can be badly misleading.

That is a procedure for trained personnel working under a written, physician-directed protocol with equipment on site. It is not a bystander technique.

Worth saying directly: a recovery cold plunge is not an emergency cooling station. If someone is confused and overheated, the answer is 911, not a walk across the building to a tub.

## Low blood sugar wears a lot of costumes

**Hypoglycemia** — blood sugar dropping too low — can look like almost anything. Shakiness, sweating, sudden irritability, confusion, poor coordination, and in some people something that reads as drunk. It happens most often in people taking insulin or certain diabetes medications, and a hard session on a skipped meal can trigger it.

If the person is awake and can swallow safely, sugar is the answer — juice, glucose tabs, regular soda. If they cannot swallow safely or are not fully awake, do not put anything in their mouth. Call 911.

## Cold has its own risks, including in a recovery room

Cold runs a spectrum too — shivering and clumsiness at the mild end, confusion and apathy as **hypothermia** deepens, and localized freezing injury as **frostbite** in exposed fingers and ears on an Indiana January run.

Cold recovery modalities carry a smaller version. Water at 38 to 42 degrees is fine for most healthy adults for short exposures, and it is uncomfortable enough that people compete over duration. That competition is where the risk lives, and cold shock makes the first thirty seconds of breathing hard to control alone.

Some people should talk to a doctor before ever getting in: cardiovascular conditions, uncontrolled high blood pressure, pregnancy, Raynaud's.

## When collapse is not about heat at all

Someone who goes down at the end of hard exercise may have simple exercise-associated collapse, which resolves lying down with the legs elevated. Two other causes matter more.

**Sudden cardiac arrest** is the leading medical cause of death in young athletes and is often mistaken for a faint or a seizure. Unresponsive and not breathing normally means cardiac arrest.

**Exertional sickling** can occur in people with sickle cell trait during intense sustained effort — weakness and muscle pain out of proportion to the work, often mistaken for cramping. Anyone carrying the trait should raise it with a physician.

## The honest limits

Recognition in the field is imperfect, and the only reliable heat stroke measurement requires equipment you do not have. Hydration advice is oversold with numbers nobody can support — sweat rates vary enormously, so generic ounce-per-hour rules are guesses. Weighing yourself before and after a hot session tells you more than any chart will.

And once more: reading this does not certify you. Hands-on certification through the American Heart Association or the American Red Cross is the version that works under pressure.
