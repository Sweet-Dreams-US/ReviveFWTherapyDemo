Sudden cardiac arrest does not look like the movies. No clutched chest, no warning. Someone finishes a set, sits down, and goes over.

Here is the uncomfortable part: the outcome is mostly determined by whoever happens to be standing nearby. Not by the paramedics. By you.

## The links in the chain, and which ones are yours

The American Heart Association describes cardiac arrest response as a **chain of survival** — a sequence where each step depends on the one before it. For an adult who collapses in public, the early links belong to bystanders, not professionals.

Recognize it and call 911. Start chest compressions. Get an AED on the chest as fast as possible. Then paramedics take over, and then the hospital does its part.

Blood stops moving the instant the heart stops, and the brain has no reserve. Compressions are a manual pump keeping some flow going until a shock can reset the rhythm. Every minute without either one lowers the odds, which is why a defibrillator on a wall thirty feet away is worth more than an ambulance six minutes out.

If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, that is the trigger. You do not need to be certain. Starting compressions on someone who turns out to have fainted is a recoverable mistake. Standing there deciding is not.

## What good compressions look like

Certification courses teach this with a mannequin and a metronome, and the details are what make it work.

The current adult guidance is to push in the center of the chest, hard and fast, at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute — roughly the tempo of a song you already know. Let the chest come all the way back up between pushes, because the heart only refills during that recoil. And stop as little as possible; every pause drops the pressure you have built back to zero.

Most untrained bystanders push too slow, too shallow, and stop too often — exactly the gap a three-hour class closes.

Hands-only CPR, compressions with no rescue breaths, is the recommended approach for untrained bystanders responding to an adult who collapses suddenly. It is simpler, people are more willing to do it, and it works.

## How an AED works, and why people are afraid of it

An automated external defibrillator is not a device that restarts a stopped heart. That framing is where the fear comes from.

What it does is interrupt a chaotic rhythm. In many cardiac arrests the heart has not stopped so much as descended into useless electrical noise, quivering without pumping. A defibrillator delivers a controlled shock that stops everything for an instant so the heart's own pacemaker has a chance to restart in an organized rhythm.

The machine decides, not you. You turn it on, put the pads where the pictures on them show, and it analyzes. If a shock is not appropriate, it will not deliver one, no matter what buttons you press. That is the single most important thing to know: **you cannot shock someone who does not need it.**

The rest is following the voice prompts. It talks you through every step in plain English, including when to stand clear and when to resume compressions.

A few situations change the details — a wet chest, which is a real scenario in a facility with cold plunges and saunas, or an implanted pacemaker under the skin. Certification covers those in about five minutes. None of them are reasons to walk away from the machine. Kids and infants have their own modifications, and those are worth learning in a class rather than from a page.

## The honest limits

Bystander CPR and early defibrillation meaningfully improve survival odds. They do not make cardiac arrest survivable in the way people expect. Many arrests are not reversible no matter how perfect the response, and someone who does everything right may still lose the person. The guilt afterward is real and usually misplaced.

The other limit: reading this does not certify you. It is orientation, not training. Hands-on certification through the American Heart Association or the American Red Cross takes a few hours and is the only version of this that lives in your hands instead of your head. Choking relief — back blows, abdominal thrusts, and what changes when the person goes unresponsive — is taught in the same class.
