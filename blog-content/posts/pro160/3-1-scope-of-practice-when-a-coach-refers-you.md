There is a moment that separates good coaches from confident ones. A member describes a symptom, and the coach either has an answer for it or says some version of "that is outside what I do, and you should get it looked at."

The second answer sounds weaker. It is not. It is the sound of someone who knows where their authority ends, which is rarer in this industry than a certification on the wall.

## Three terms that decide what a coach can legally do

**Scope of practice** is the set of activities a professional is trained, credentialed, and legally permitted to perform. For licensed professions the state defines it in statute. For unlicensed ones it is drawn by credential requirements, industry standards, and what the law reserves for licensees.

**Standard of care** is what a reasonably prudent professional with similar training would do in the same situation. It is the yardstick, and it is not set by the individual.

**Negligence** is what happens when the standard is not met and someone gets hurt. It has four parts: a duty to the person, a failure to meet the standard, a link between that failure and the harm, and actual damages. All four have to be there.

The practical consequence is that a coach who steps outside their scope has already handed away the argument that they met the standard of care. Practicing outside your lane is not just a professional problem. It is the legal problem.

## What a personal trainer is, legally

Here is what most members do not know: no U.S. state licenses personal trainers, and Indiana is no exception. There is no state exam, no registry, and no board that can pull anyone's credential.

What exists instead are private certifications. The reputable ones require an accredited exam, current CPR certification, and continuing education — accreditation through the National Commission for Certifying Agencies is the usual marker. The disreputable ones are a weekend and a PDF. Both can accurately say they are "certified."

Indiana does license or regulate the professions around fitness — physicians, physical therapists, chiropractors, athletic trainers, massage therapists, mental health professionals — and those licenses carry legally protected activities. That boundary is the whole map. A trainer's scope is defined largely by what other people's licenses reserve.

## Four things that are always out of bounds

**Diagnosis.** A coach cannot tell you that you have a labral tear, plantar fasciitis, low testosterone, or an inflamed disc. They can say what they observe and where it points, then send you to someone who can name it.

**Treatment and treatment claims.** Prescribing exercise for general fitness is coaching. Prescribing exercise to treat a diagnosed condition is rehabilitation, and it belongs to licensed clinicians. The same goes for any claim that a service treats, cures, or heals something.

**Prescription of anything.** Medications obviously, but also the softer version — telling you to stop a medication, adjust a dose, or use a supplement as though it were a drug for a diagnosed condition.

**Joint manipulation.** High-velocity adjustments, and most hands-on techniques that move a joint through its end range for therapeutic purposes, are reserved activities. "Popping your back" as a favor between sets is not a gray area.

## Working under someone else's license

There is a legitimate version of a coach doing rehab-adjacent work, and it involves someone else's license. A physical therapist or physician directs a plan, and a fitness professional carries out the parts delegated to them, within the limits that provider sets and with communication running both directions. That is supervision and delegation, and it produces the best outcomes for people coming out of an injury.

What it is not is a coach reading a discharge summary and inventing the rest. The difference is whether the licensed provider is actually in the loop.

## The honest limits

The boundary is fuzzier in practice than on paper. Where exactly "coaching someone with a bad shoulder" becomes "treating a shoulder injury" depends on the state, the situation, and the relationship between the professionals involved.

And staying inside your scope does not make you good at your job. Plenty of coaches never cross a legal line and still write bad programs. Scope is a floor, not a standard of excellence.

What you can reasonably expect is that the person coaching you knows the boundary exists, says so out loud when they reach it, and has names to give you. If you have never once heard your coach say "that is not mine," it is worth wondering why.
