You finish a heavy set of squats, rack the bar, stand up, and the edges of the room go gray for a second or two. Or you step out of the sauna, stand, and have to put a hand on the wall.

That isn't weakness and it isn't a mystery. It's a control system that works well but works with a delay — and the delay is exactly long enough to notice.

## Blood flow is a pressure problem, not a pumping problem

Your heart supplies pressure. What decides where blood actually goes is resistance — how wide or narrow each vessel is.

Flow through a tube changes with the fourth power of its radius. Cut the radius in half and flow drops to a sixteenth of what it was.

That's why the arterioles, the small arteries feeding your tissues, are the control valves of the whole system. They're wrapped in smooth muscle held at a constant partial squeeze — vasomotor tone — and adjusting it is how your body sends blood toward working muscle and away from your gut during training, without the heart doing anything different.

## The reflex that catches you when you stand

Two patches of stretch-sensitive nerve endings, one in the carotid arteries and one at the arch of the aorta, read arterial pressure continuously. They're baroreceptors, and they fire more when the wall stretches more.

Stand up quickly and gravity drops a large volume of blood into your legs before your veins react. Filling pressure falls, the next stroke is smaller, and pressure at your neck drops. The baroreceptors go quiet, and that silence tells the brainstem to speed the heart and tighten the arterioles until pressure returns.

The loop takes a couple of heartbeats. The gray-out lives in that gap.

Anything that reduces the fluid in your system widens the gap: a hard session, a humid Fort Wayne July, and especially heat, which dilates skin vessels and pulls blood toward the surface. Stand up out of the cedar sauna after hard training and you've stacked three of them.

Over hours, a slower system takes over: your kidneys release renin, starting the chain that produces angiotensin and aldosterone, which tighten vessels and hold onto sodium and water. That defends blood volume across a day and is useless in the two seconds you needed help.

## Why your ankles swell and your hands puff

At the capillary, two pressures argue. Blood pressure inside pushes fluid out into the tissue. Proteins in the blood, mostly albumin, pull it back in by osmosis. These are the Starling forces, and the balance normally leaks slightly more out than comes back. Lymphatic vessels collect the leftover and return it to circulation.

Stand all day and pressure inside the capillaries in your feet rises, more fluid is pushed out than the lymphatics can carry, and your socks leave marks. The lymphatic system has no pump of its own. It moves because you move.

The pump you feel during a set is the same physics running fast: dilated vessels, high local pressure, fluid pushed into the muscle. Real, temporary, and not growth.

## What training does to your blood

Endurance work expands plasma volume within days — more fluid, more filling, more blood per beat. It also raises total red cell mass, but more slowly.

That creates a confusing lab result. Because plasma expands faster than red cells do, a well-trained endurance athlete can show a low hemoglobin concentration while carrying more total oxygen-carrying capacity than before. That's dilution, sometimes called sports anemia, and it isn't true anemia. Only a physician looking at the full picture can tell those apart, and low iron is common enough that guessing is a bad idea.

Here's the honest limit on the recovery side. Pneumatic compression does move fluid and it reliably makes legs feel better. What it does not do is flush lactic acid — lactate clears on its own within about an hour of stopping and isn't what makes you sore two days later. Most of the research measures how sore people report feeling, which is worth something but isn't the same as recovering faster, and you can't blind anyone to whether their legs are in a boot.

One thing here is not a training topic. Brief lightheadedness on standing is common. Fainting, near-fainting that repeats, dizziness with chest pain or pressure, a pounding or irregular heartbeat, or trouble breathing are not things to work around or hydrate away. Stop and see a physician. Severe or sudden symptoms mean emergency care, immediately.
