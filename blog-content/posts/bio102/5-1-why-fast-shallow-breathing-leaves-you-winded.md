At the top of a hard set of sled pushes, breathing gets fast and shallow all by itself. It feels like your body's best attempt at getting air. It's actually one of the least efficient patterns available.

Understanding why means knowing something most people have backwards. You don't pull air into your lungs. You make room, and the atmosphere does the rest.

## You don't suck air in, you make space for it

Your lungs have no muscle of their own. They're passive bags that follow whatever the chest wall does, and the reason they follow is a thin film of fluid in the pleural space between them.

That space sits at a slightly negative pressure, lower than the pressure inside the airways. The difference across the lung wall, called transpulmonary pressure, is what holds the lungs open against their own tendency to collapse.

When your diaphragm drops and your ribs swing up and out, the chest cavity gets bigger. Pleural pressure falls further, the lungs are pulled wider, alveolar pressure drops below atmospheric, and air flows down the gradient into you. Exhaling at rest takes no work at all: the tissue recoils, pressure reverses, air leaves.

Air moves from high pressure to low. Breathing is the business of creating the low.

## Two things make that job harder

**Stiffness.** Compliance is how easily the lungs stretch for a given effort. Some of that is the tissue itself, but a bigger share is surface tension inside hundreds of millions of wet alveoli, each of which would rather collapse. Cells in the alveolar wall secrete surfactant, a detergent-like substance that disrupts the water film and drops surface tension sharply. Without it, every breath would take enormous force.

**Narrowness.** Resistance to airflow depends almost entirely on airway diameter, and small changes matter a lot — halve the radius of a tube and flow through it drops to a fraction of what it was. This is why anything that narrows the airways — smooth muscle constriction, inflammation, swelling — is felt so sharply. In Fort Wayne, cold dry January air is a common trigger for the airway tightening some people get during exercise.

That last one is a medical conversation, not a coaching one. Wheezing, chest tightness, coughing after training, waking up short of breath, or breathlessness out of proportion to what you're doing all need a physician's evaluation. Don't train through them and don't borrow someone's inhaler. If you're struggling to breathe right now, that is emergency care.

## "Increasing your lung capacity" is mostly a myth

Spirometry measures how much air you can move and how fast. Tidal volume is a normal quiet breath. Vital capacity is everything you can exhale after the deepest breath you can take. Residual volume is what stays behind no matter what, because your lungs never fully empty.

Here's the part the fitness industry gets wrong. In healthy adults, endurance training does not meaningfully increase vital capacity or total lung capacity. Lung size is set largely by body size, age, and genetics. What improves with training is everything downstream: your heart moves more blood per beat, your muscles build capillaries and mitochondria, and your breathing muscles get stronger. The lungs were rarely the bottleneck.

So when someone sells a device that will expand your lung capacity, the honest version is narrower. Inspiratory muscle training can strengthen the muscles you breathe with, and it may reduce how hard breathing feels. That's real but modest, and it is not the same as making your lungs bigger. Anyone promising the second thing is selling the first.

## The dead space problem

This is the answer to the sled push.

Every breath has to fill your mouth, throat, trachea, and bronchi before any air reaches an alveolus. Those passages conduct air but don't exchange gas — anatomical dead space, a fixed volume that doesn't change with how you breathe.

Say dead space is roughly a third of a normal quiet breath. Take a normal breath and two-thirds of that air reaches gas-exchanging tissue. Now halve the breath and double the rate. Same total air per minute, but dead space eats a much larger share of every breath, and the amount reaching your alveoli falls. You're working harder for less exchange.

Then there's the cost of the work itself. Breathing muscles use oxygen and demand blood flow. Fast, shallow breathing raises that cost while lowering the return.

The fix is unglamorous. When you're winded, slow the rate a notch and make the breaths deeper. Same air per minute, more of it reaching the places that matter, less panic.

## The Revive Tip

Practice deep breathing somewhere calm before you need it at the top of a hard interval. The cedar sauna is the best place we have for it: heat already slows you down, so sit tall, let your belly move first and your ribs second, and hold a slow four-count in with a longer count out.
