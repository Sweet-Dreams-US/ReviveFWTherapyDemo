There is a box of electrolyte packets in your gym bag, and you are not entirely sure why. Someone said they help with cramps. Someone else said the salt is the point. A third person said you are chronically dehydrated and do not know it.

Meanwhile you are tired most afternoons and have quietly wondered whether it is your iron. Minerals are where nutrition gets simultaneously the most measurable and the most marketed. It is worth knowing which parts are settled science and which parts are a wrapper.

## The minerals you need in gram amounts

Seven minerals are classified as major, meaning your body needs them in quantities large enough to measure in grams: calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, potassium, chloride, and sulfur.

**Calcium** is the one everybody associates with bone, and roughly all of your body's calcium is in your skeleton. What gets missed is that the small remaining fraction runs muscle contraction and nerve signaling, and your body defends that blood level ferociously — pulling from bone when intake is short. That is why a normal blood calcium result tells you almost nothing about whether your intake is adequate. The number stays normal until things are quite wrong.

**Phosphorus** partners with calcium in bone and forms the backbone of ATP, the molecule your cells actually spend to do work. It is abundant in food and shortfalls are uncommon.

**Magnesium** is a cofactor in hundreds of enzyme reactions, and ATP is biologically active bound to magnesium — the working form is magnesium-ATP. It also participates in muscle relaxation, which is the origin of every magnesium-for-cramps claim you have seen. Intakes below recommended levels are genuinely common in the United States, which makes magnesium one of the more defensible things on a supplement shelf. It is also not a cure for cramps, and the trial evidence for that specific use is weak.

**Sodium, potassium, and chloride** are the electrolytes proper. They carry electrical charge across cell membranes, and the sodium-potassium pump — which burns a substantial share of your resting energy every day — is what keeps the gradient that lets nerves fire and muscles contract. Most Americans take in plenty of sodium and less potassium than recommended, which is roughly the reverse of what a gym-bag electrolyte marketing pitch implies.

## Trace minerals: tiny amounts, narrow windows

Iron, zinc, copper, selenium, iodine, manganese, chromium, and fluoride are needed in milligram or microgram amounts. Small requirement does not mean small consequence, and several of them have a narrow gap between not enough and too much.

**Zinc** supports immune function, wound healing, and hundreds of enzymes. High-dose zinc supplementation taken for months interferes with copper absorption and can produce a copper deficiency — a good example of how a single-nutrient megadose creates a new problem.

**Selenium** is built into glutathione peroxidase, one of your antioxidant enzymes. It also has one of the narrower upper limits in the whole set. Brazil nuts are extraordinarily concentrated in it, which is why the folk advice to eat a handful daily is worse advice than it sounds.

**Iodine** is a structural component of thyroid hormone, full stop — you cannot build the hormone without it. Iodized salt was one of the most effective public health measures of the last century. Worth knowing: most specialty salts, including sea salt and kosher salt, are not iodized, and neither is the salt in most processed food.

**Chromium and manganese** show up constantly in metabolism supplements. The evidence behind those claims is thin.

## Why iron is the one to test rather than guess

Iron sits at the center of oxygen transport, and iron deficiency is one of the most common nutritional problems worldwide. It is also the mineral most likely to be self-diagnosed and self-treated badly.

Dietary iron comes in two forms. **Heme iron** — from meat, poultry, and fish — is absorbed through its own pathway at a relatively high and stable rate. **Non-heme iron** — from plants, eggs, and supplements — is far more variable, and its absorption depends heavily on what is on the plate with it.

Vitamin C substantially improves non-heme absorption. So does eating it alongside meat. Working against it: phytates in whole grains and legumes, polyphenols in tea and coffee, and calcium. Coffee with breakfast and an iron-rich meal is a real interaction, not a hack from a wellness blog.

Your body has no meaningful way to excrete excess iron. It regulates absorption instead, through a hormone called hepcidin that clamps down when stores are full. That is exactly why unsupervised iron supplementation is a bad idea: taking iron you do not need means it accumulates, and iron overload damages organs. People with hereditary hemochromatosis, which is not rare, can be harmed by supplements sold over the counter.

**Ferritin** is the storage protein that most panels use to estimate iron reserves, and it has one significant catch. Ferritin is also an acute-phase reactant, meaning inflammation, infection, and a hard training block can all push it up. A ferritin value that looks reassuring during an inflammatory period can hide low stores underneath.

Endurance athletes, people who menstruate, and people who have recently donated blood carry higher risk. Distance running adds foot-strike hemolysis and an exercise-driven rise in hepcidin. If you are dragging, that is a reason to ask a physician for a full iron panel — ferritin interpreted with other markers, not alone — rather than a reason to start a supplement.

## How much water do you actually need

The eight-glasses rule has no strong scientific origin. It has simply been repeated long enough to feel like a finding.

Your body regulates fluid tightly. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus sense the concentration of your blood and trigger both thirst and the release of antidiuretic hormone, which tells your kidneys to hold onto water. For most healthy adults in normal circumstances, drinking to thirst plus the water in food does the job.

Where individual variation gets large is sweat. Sweat rate differs enormously between people and between days — a summer humid morning at 75 percent humidity is a completely different fluid situation than a January session in a cool gym. Sweat sodium concentration also varies widely between individuals. Some people finish a long run with visible salt crust on their face; others do not.

That is where electrolyte products earn their place: long sessions, hot conditions, heavy sweaters, and repeated same-day training. For a 45-minute indoor lifting session in air conditioning, water is generally fine, and the packet is mostly flavor.

Overdrinking is a real hazard in the other direction. Exercise-associated hyponatremia — dangerously diluted blood sodium — happens when people drink large volumes of plain water during long events, and it is more dangerous than mild dehydration. Urine color is a rough gauge, though B vitamin supplements and beets will throw it off.

The honest summary is that hydration status is highly individual, hard to measure well outside a lab, and mostly self-correcting if you have fluid available and pay attention to thirst.
