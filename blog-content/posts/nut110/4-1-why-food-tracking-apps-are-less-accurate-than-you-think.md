You tracked everything for three weeks. Weighed some of it, guessed at the rest, logged the coffee, mostly logged the weekend. The numbers looked reasonable. Nothing happened.

The reflex is to conclude something is wrong with your metabolism. Usually something much more ordinary is going on: measuring what a human being eats is one of the hardest measurement problems in all of science, and the app on your phone is not solving it. Researchers who do this for a living know exactly how unreliable the data is, because they have spent decades checking it against objective methods.

## The four ways anyone measures what a person eats

Every dietary assessment method in existence is a variation on four approaches, and each one trades accuracy against feasibility.

**The 24-hour recall.** A trained interviewer walks you through everything you consumed yesterday, in order, with probing questions about preparation and portion size. It does not change your behavior, because it happens after the fact. It is limited by memory, and one day is a poor picture of how you usually eat — most people need multiple non-consecutive days, including a weekend day, before the average means anything.

**The food frequency questionnaire.** A long checklist asking how often you ate each of a hundred-plus foods over the past several months. It is cheap, scalable, and the reason large nutrition studies exist at all. It is also coarse. Portion categories are broad, memory over months is worse than memory over a day, and the resulting numbers carry substantial error.

**The food record.** You write down what you eat as you eat it, usually for three to seven days. Being prospective removes the memory problem but introduces a worse one: **reactivity**. People change what they eat because they are recording it. Simpler meals, fewer snacks, less alcohol. You end up documenting a slightly fictional version of your normal week.

**Weighed intake.** Every item on a scale before it goes in your mouth. This is the most accurate approach available outside a metabolic ward, and almost nobody sustains it beyond a few days.

Researchers know how much all of these methods miss because they have an objective comparator. **Doubly labeled water** measures actual energy expenditure over one to two weeks by tracking how quickly two harmless isotopes leave the body. In a weight-stable person, expenditure equals intake. Compare that to what people reported eating and the gap is consistent and one-directional.

## Everybody underreports, and it isn't lying

This is the most robust finding in the whole field, and the most misunderstood. Underreporting of energy intake shows up in nearly every population studied. It is systematic, not random. And it is not primarily dishonesty.

Some of it is memory — you genuinely do not remember the handful of pretzels at 3 p.m. Some of it is portion estimation, which is very difficult even for trained professionals. Restaurant portions defeat almost everyone.

Some of it is what researchers call selective underreporting: the foods that go missing from logs are not random. Snacks, added fats, sauces, alcohol, and weekend eating disappear at higher rates than breakfast cereal does. Weighing your chicken and eyeballing your olive oil produces a very specific kind of error, since a few unmeasured tablespoons of oil carry more energy than most people assume.

And some of it is not a reporting error at all — people eat less during the period they are recording, and then return to normal afterward.

None of this makes tracking worthless. It makes tracking a lens rather than a ledger. Relative change tells you something. Absolute numbers do not deserve the confidence the app's decimal places imply.

## What the app is actually pulling from

There are two very different kinds of nutrition database, and most consumer apps blend them without telling you.

The gold standard is a curated food composition database — in the United States, **USDA FoodData Central**, which draws on laboratory analysis and manufacturer submissions. Even there, a single food is a range, not a value. Two apples differ. Beef differs by cut, grade, and trim. Cooking changes water content, which changes everything per gram.

The other kind is crowd-sourced. Users add entries, and nobody verifies them. Search a common food in most popular apps and you will find a dozen entries with meaningfully different values for the same thing. Whichever one you tap becomes your data.

Packaged food has its own slack built into the label by regulation. Serving sizes are defined by rule and may not match how anyone eats the product. Rounding rules allow small amounts to be reported as zero — trans fat below half a gram per serving can legally be listed as 0 grams, and a product with several servings of that adds up to a number that never appeared on the panel. None of this is fraud. It is what the labeling system permits.

## What a real assessment actually looks like

Clinicians do not evaluate nutrition from a food log alone. They use a framework abbreviated **ABCD**, and dietary data is only the last letter.

**Anthropometric** — body measurements and how they change over time.
**Biochemical** — laboratory values, from iron studies to metabolic panels.
**Clinical** — physical signs, medical history, medications, symptoms, surgeries, and conditions that change what a person absorbs.
**Dietary** — what the person reports eating.

The point of the framework is triangulation. Any single leg is weak. A ferritin value means one thing during an inflammatory episode and another thing at rest. A food log means one thing from someone with no reason to shade it and another thing from someone under pressure to look consistent. Put four imperfect sources together and the picture stabilizes.

That is also the honest reason a registered dietitian is a different job from a coach. Reading biochemical data and building nutrition care around a diagnosed condition takes clinical training and credentialing. A coach can talk about food. A coach should not be interpreting your labs.

## Where the honest limits are

Tracking is a skill-building tool with a real cost, and it is not right for everyone.

For some people it builds genuine awareness in a few weeks and can then be put down. For others, particularly anyone with a history of disordered eating, logging food creates a rigid relationship with numbers that makes things worse. If tracking makes you anxious, if a missed entry ruins your day, or if you find yourself eating around what is easy to log, that is a signal worth taking to a physician or an eating-disorder-informed dietitian rather than pushing through.

And a note on the entire genre of headlines built on this data. When you read that a study found a particular food associated with an outcome, remember that the exposure was very often measured with a food frequency questionnaire filled out from memory. That does not make the finding worthless. It does explain why nutrition headlines contradict each other so reliably.

## The Revive Tip

Try a shorter, more honest version. Instead of logging everything indefinitely, pick four days — two weekdays and two weekend days — and write down what you eat with no attempt to change it. The goal is not a calorie number. The goal is pattern recognition: when you eat, what is missing, what the drive home from work does to your evening.

Then bring the pattern, not the spreadsheet, to a conversation with someone who trains people. Four days of honest notes is more useful than four months of half-logged data.
