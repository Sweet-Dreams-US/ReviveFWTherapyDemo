"Carbs" might be the least useful word in nutrition. It covers table sugar and it covers the fiber in a bowl of black beans — molecules that share a chemical family and then do almost nothing alike once they are past your teeth.

Whole eating patterns get built on that single word, in both directions. It is worth knowing what is actually inside it, because the differences between the members of this family are larger than the differences between the family and the other macronutrients.

## What the word actually covers

Carbohydrates are classified by how many sugar units are linked together.

**Monosaccharides** are single units: glucose, fructose, and galactose. Glucose is the currency your bloodstream runs on and the fuel your brain prefers.

**Disaccharides** are two units joined. Sucrose is glucose plus fructose — table sugar. Lactose is glucose plus galactose — milk sugar. Maltose is two glucose.

**Oligosaccharides** are short chains, typically three to ten units. Raffinose and stachyose in beans, and fructans in wheat, onions, and garlic, live here. Humans do not produce the enzymes to break several of these apart, so they arrive in the colon intact and get fermented by bacteria. That is the bean problem, and it is also the entire basis of FODMAP sensitivity.

**Polysaccharides** are long chains. Starch is the plant storage form. Glycogen is the animal storage form, stored in your liver and muscle. Most dietary fibers are polysaccharides your enzymes cannot dismantle.

Notice that "simple" and "complex" do not appear on that list. They are useful shorthand for a conversation and nearly useless for predicting what a food does, because a complex carbohydrate in white bread behaves more like sugar than the sugar in an apple does.

## Starch, glycogen, and the several different things called fiber

**Starch** comes in two architectures. Amylose is a straight chain and digests relatively slowly. Amylopectin is heavily branched, which gives enzymes far more places to attack, so it digests fast. The ratio varies by plant, and then cooking, cooling, grinding, and ripeness all shift it further. Cooked and cooled starch forms resistant starch, which partially escapes digestion and behaves more like fiber.

**Glycogen** is your stored carbohydrate, and its two pools do different jobs. Liver glycogen can be released back into the bloodstream to defend blood glucose between meals. Muscle glycogen cannot. It lacks the enzyme required to export glucose, so muscle glycogen fuels only the muscle it is sitting in. That is why the last third of a long conditioning piece or a Hyrox-style workout feels the way it does — you are draining a local tank that nothing else can refill mid-session.

**Fiber** is not one substance, and grouping it as a single number on a label hides most of what matters.

- *Soluble and viscous* fibers — oat beta-glucan, psyllium, pectin — dissolve into a gel. They slow gastric emptying, blunt the rise in blood glucose after a meal, and bind bile acids in the gut.
- *Insoluble* fibers — wheat bran, the structural material in vegetables — add bulk and influence transit time.
- *Fermentable* fibers get consumed by colonic bacteria, which produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate that feed the cells lining your colon.

A real food contains a mix, in proportions the label does not tell you. What is not in dispute is that most American adults take in substantially less fiber than dietary guidance recommends, and it is one of the few nutrition gaps where the evidence for closing it is broad and consistent.

## How carbohydrate actually gets from your plate into your blood

Digestion starts in the mouth, where salivary amylase begins cleaving starch, and then stops in the stomach when acid shuts that enzyme down. The real work happens in the small intestine, where pancreatic amylase breaks starch into short fragments and brush-border enzymes — maltase, sucrase, and lactase — finish the job down to single sugars.

Only single sugars can cross. Glucose and galactose enter through **SGLT1**, a sodium-dependent cotransporter that rides the sodium gradient to pull sugar in even against its concentration gradient. Fructose enters separately through **GLUT5**, a facilitated transporter that does not use energy, works more slowly, and saturates. Both then exit the intestinal cell into the bloodstream through GLUT2.

Two practical things fall directly out of that plumbing.

First, a large fructose load can outrun GLUT5 capacity, leaving unabsorbed fructose to draw water into the gut and get fermented. That is why a big glass of juice or a fructose-heavy gel sits badly in some people during hard training.

Second, glucose and fructose use different doors. Sports products that combine both can deliver more total carbohydrate per hour than glucose alone, which is the actual mechanism behind multiple-transportable-carbohydrate formulas. That only becomes relevant during continuous hard efforts lasting beyond roughly an hour. For a 45-minute lift, it is a solution to a problem you do not have.

Worth adding: most of the world's adults produce less lactase after childhood. That is the ancestral human default, not a disease, and the ability to digest lactose into adulthood is the genetic exception.

## Glycemic index, glycemic load, and where both fall apart

The **glycemic index** ranks how much a fixed amount of available carbohydrate from a given food raises blood glucose over two hours, compared against a reference. **Glycemic load** scales that ranking by the portion you actually eat, which makes it more sensible — a food can rank high and still contribute little if a normal serving contains almost no carbohydrate.

Now the limitations, which are documented and substantial.

GI values are generated in a lab, in fasted people, from single foods eaten alone. You almost never eat that way. Add fat, protein, acid, or fiber to the same meal and the response changes. Ripeness, variety, particle size, cooking method, and how long the food sat after cooking all move the value for what the label calls the same food.

And the response varies enormously between people. The same bread produces meaningfully different glucose curves in different individuals, and even in the same person on different days depending on sleep, prior meals, stress, and recent training.

This is where the honest limit belongs, because it is a fast-moving marketing category. Continuous glucose monitors are now sold to people without diabetes on the promise of personalized precision. The underlying measurement is real and the between-person variation is real. What has not been established is that chasing flatter glucose curves improves health outcomes in metabolically healthy people. It is genuinely interesting data being sold with premature certainty. If you have diabetes or prediabetes, CGM use is a clinical decision made with your physician, and that is a different conversation entirely.

What survives all of this is unglamorous: the physical form of the food, how much fiber came with it, and what else is on the plate predict your response better than any number on a chart.
