Two miles into a run that was going fine, everything changes and the only thought in your head is where the nearest bathroom is. Or you load up a heavy set of squats and get a burn behind your sternum that has nothing to do with your legs.

Neither of those is a character flaw or a sign you ate something bad. Both are predictable consequences of what hard training does to a digestive system that was not designed to run at full speed while you do.

## What actually happens between your mouth and everything else

Digestion is two processes running at once. **Mechanical digestion** breaks food into smaller pieces. **Chemical digestion** breaks the chemical bonds inside those pieces so the fragments are small enough to cross into your bloodstream.

It starts in your mouth, where chewing does the mechanical part and salivary amylase begins on starch. Your stomach is a mixing tank: it churns, adds hydrochloric acid strong enough to denature protein, and activates pepsin to start cutting protein chains. What leaves is a semi-liquid called chyme, released into the small intestine in controlled squirts rather than all at once.

The small intestine is where almost all absorption happens, and it is built for surface area. Its inner wall folds, the folds are covered in finger-like villi, and every cell on those villi carries a brush border of microvilli. The result is an absorptive surface far larger than the organ's dimensions suggest.

By the time material reaches your colon, most of the calories are gone. What is left there is water reclamation and fermentation — and fermentation turns out to be a bigger deal than anyone thought thirty years ago.

Timing is worth a mental note. A mixed meal typically clears the stomach in a couple of hours, spends several more in the small intestine, and then sits in the colon far longer than either — total transit for most people is measured in a day or more, with wide individual variation. That is why the thing bothering you mid-run is rarely what you ate an hour ago.

## The chemical messages that control the timing

Your gut runs on hormones that you never notice until the timing goes wrong.

**Gastrin** signals your stomach to produce acid when food arrives. **Secretin** tells your pancreas to release bicarbonate, neutralizing the acid so intestinal enzymes can work. **Cholecystokinin**, usually written CCK, is released when fat and protein hit the small intestine. It squeezes the gallbladder to release bile, calls for pancreatic enzymes, and slows how fast your stomach empties.

That last function is the one you feel. Bile is not an enzyme — it is an emulsifier, breaking fat into droplets small enough for enzymes to work on, the same way dish soap breaks up grease. Fat requires that whole process, so a high-fat meal leaves your stomach slowly.

Which is precisely why a burger sits like a brick when you train ninety minutes later, while toast does not. It is not that fat is bad before training. It is that fat is slow before training.

## Your gut bacteria, minus the marketing

Your colon hosts a large microbial community that ferments what your own enzymes could not break down — mostly fiber and resistant starch.

The main products are **short-chain fatty acids**: acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Butyrate is notable because it is the preferred fuel of the cells lining your colon. Your gut bacteria are, in a literal sense, feeding part of your gut wall.

Two terms get used as if they were the same thing. A **prebiotic** is food for those bacteria — fermentable fiber from onions, garlic, oats, legumes, slightly green bananas. A **probiotic** is live organisms you swallow. Feeding the residents is not the same as importing new ones.

Here is where honesty is required. The microbiome is a young field producing genuinely exciting research, and consumer products have sprinted far ahead of what the science supports. Probiotic effects are strain-specific — a benefit shown for one particular strain in one particular condition does not transfer to a different strain in a different bottle, and most retail products do not carry strain-level evidence for what the box implies. Direct-to-consumer microbiome test kits will happily sell you a personalized report that the underlying science cannot currently support.

What is well supported is far less exciting and much cheaper: people who eat more fiber from more varied plant sources tend to have a more diverse gut community. That is the intervention with actual evidence behind it.

One practical caution if you decide to act on that. Increasing fiber quickly gives your bacteria far more to ferment than they are used to, and the gas and bloating that follow are a fermentation problem, not an allergy. Ramping up over several weeks rather than several days spares you the misery and the false conclusion that beans are your enemy.

## Why training makes all of this worse

Gastrointestinal complaints are extremely common in active people, and there are three mechanisms doing most of the damage.

**Blood goes elsewhere.** During hard exercise, your body redirects blood flow toward working muscle and skin, and away from your digestive organs. A gut running on reduced blood flow digests poorly and, when the effort is long and hot enough, becomes more permeable. Heat makes it worse, which is why summer sessions produce more problems than winter ones.

**Mechanical jostling.** Running shakes your intestinal contents for the entire duration. Cycling and lifting do not, which is why runners report far more of this than lifters do.

**Pressure.** Heavy lifting means high intra-abdominal pressure, often with a hard brace and sometimes with your torso bent over. That is a setup for reflux — stomach contents pushed up past a valve that is under more pressure than it was built for. Timing meals further from heavy sessions and avoiding large volumes of liquid right before lifting are the usual first adjustments.

There is one genuinely encouraging finding buried in this. The gut is trainable. Endurance athletes who practice taking in carbohydrate during long sessions tolerate more of it over time, apparently through changes in gastric emptying and in the density of intestinal transporters. If you have decided you cannot eat before or during training, it may be that you have never systematically practiced it. Start small and repeat it on easy sessions rather than testing something new on a hard one.

Then there are the food-specific issues. **Lactose intolerance** is the normal adult condition for most of the world's population — lactase production declines after childhood, undigested lactose pulls water into the intestine, and colonic bacteria ferment it into gas. **FODMAPs** are a group of fermentable carbohydrates that produce similar symptoms in sensitive people.

An important caution on that last one. A low-FODMAP protocol is a structured diagnostic elimination with a planned reintroduction phase, designed to be run with a dietitian. It was never meant to be a permanent way of eating, and used as one it needlessly narrows your diet and starves the very bacteria you want to keep.

And some symptoms are not a food problem at all. Blood in your stool, unintentional weight loss, persistent pain, trouble swallowing, or symptoms that wake you at night are reasons to see a physician. Those are not solved by swapping oat milk for almond milk.

## The Revive Tip

If your training gets derailed by your gut, run the timing experiment before you run the elimination diet.

The fix is usually boring: push the last real meal further from the session, keep pre-training food lower in fat and fiber, and stop trying to hydrate all at once in the ten minutes before you start.

Recovery timing matters too. A cedar sauna session or a cold plunge right after a large meal is uncomfortable for the same blood-flow reasons — your body is trying to do two things at once.
