Somebody on the platform next to you swears by a supplement. Somebody in your group chat has gone carnivore. Somebody at work has not eaten before noon in six months and looks great, and you cannot tell whether that is the eating window or the fact that they also started lifting.

Nutrition is the highest-noise category on the internet, and most of the noise is not lying — it is unfalsifiable, unregulated, or true in a narrower sense than it sounds. Here is how the professionals sort it.

## The popular diets agree more than the arguments suggest

Look at the dietary patterns with the most research behind them and the overlap is more striking than the differences.

**The Mediterranean pattern** is the most studied dietary pattern in existence — heavy on vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, and olive oil, light on red and processed meat. It is worth knowing that even its flagship trial, PREDIMED, had to be retracted and republished after randomization problems were found at some study sites. The re-analysis held up. That episode is a good reminder that even the best evidence in nutrition is messier than the summary articles imply.

**DASH** was developed and tested specifically in trials examining blood pressure, and it emphasizes produce, low-fat dairy, and reduced sodium.

**Vegetarian and vegan patterns** can be built well or badly, like any other. What is not optional is vitamin B12, which does not occur in plant foods in a reliable form — anyone eating fully plant-based needs a supplemental or fortified source. Iron, zinc, iodine, and long-chain omega-3s deserve attention too.

**Low-carbohydrate approaches** work well for plenty of people. What controlled feeding studies have not found is a meaningful metabolic advantage when protein and total energy are matched. The results come mostly from appetite and adherence, which is not a knock — appetite and adherence are what actually determine whether an eating pattern lasts.

**Time-restricted eating** has largely shown the same thing. Trials comparing it against a matched-intake diet have generally not found an independent advantage. For many people it reduces intake simply by shrinking the window, and that is a legitimate reason to use it.

The common thread across all of them: mostly whole foods, more fiber, adequate protein, less alcohol and heavily processed food, and a structure the person can actually live with. Adherence beats optimization. The diet you follow for two years outperforms the theoretically superior one you quit in March.

## What the law actually requires of a supplement

This is the single most useful thing to know about the industry, and most people have it backwards.

Under the **Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994** — DSHEA — supplements in the United States are regulated as a category of food, not as drugs. That means no pre-market approval. A product does not have to be shown safe or effective before it is sold. The manufacturer is responsible for making sure it is safe, and the FDA generally acts after a product is already on shelves and problems have surfaced.

The claims are shaped by the same law. Manufacturers may make **structure/function claims** — that an ingredient "supports immune function," "promotes joint comfort," "helps maintain healthy energy levels." They may not claim to treat, cure, or prevent a disease. That is why the fine print on the bottle says the statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. That disclaimer is not boilerplate. It is a legal marker telling you exactly how much verification stands behind the sentence above it.

Manufacturing rules do exist — current Good Manufacturing Practices cover identity, purity, and labeling accuracy — but compliance varies, and enforcement is complaint-driven. Independent testing over the years has repeatedly found products that do not match their label. The categories where the FDA finds adulteration with actual pharmaceutical ingredients most often are consistent and predictable: weight loss, sexual enhancement, and bodybuilding products.

## The one label check worth making

There is a shortcut, and it is a seal.

**NSF Certified for Sport**, **Informed Sport**, and **USP Verified** are third-party programs that test finished products. They verify that what is on the label is in the bottle, that the amounts are close to stated, and — in the two sport programs — that the product has been screened for substances banned in competition. Certified for Sport in particular tests lot by lot.

Be precise about what this does not mean. Third-party certification says nothing about whether the product works. A useless ingredient can be certified as accurately labeled and free of contaminants. The seal is a purity and honesty check, not an efficacy check.

If you are drug tested for any reason — competitive sport, military service, some employers, some professions — the seal is not optional. Contaminated supplements have ended careers, and "I did not know it was in there" has never once been an accepted defense.

## Who is actually allowed to tell you what to eat

The credential worth knowing is **registered dietitian**, written RD or RDN. It requires an accredited degree program, a supervised practice internship, a national examination, and ongoing continuing education. It is the credential that comes with clinical training and accountability.

"Nutritionist" is a different situation. In many places it is not a protected title, and the training behind it ranges from a graduate degree to a weekend certificate bought online. The word tells you very little on its own.

Scope of practice is set state by state, and the laws differ. The line generally falls at **medical nutrition therapy** — nutrition care designed around a diagnosed medical condition — which is reserved for credentialed and licensed practitioners. General nutrition information sits on the other side of that line: what fiber does, why protein matters, how to read a label, what the evidence says about a dietary pattern.

Practically, a coach can teach you nutrition science and help you build habits. A coach should not be designing an eating plan around your diabetes, your kidney function, your thyroid medication, or an eating disorder. If someone at any gym offers to do that, the correct response is to leave and call your physician for a referral.

## The honest part

Even the supplements with the best evidence behind them do less than the marketing suggests. Creatine monohydrate and caffeine are among the most-studied performance aids in existence, and their effects are real, measurable, and modest. Protein powder is a convenience food — useful if it helps you hit adequate protein, unnecessary if food already does. Vitamin D matters if your level is low, and finding out requires a blood test rather than a guess.

Most of the rest of the shelf is supported by mechanism arguments, cell studies, or small trials that have not replicated. That is not the same as evidence that it works, and a persuasive mechanism is the easiest thing in the world to write.

Four questions handle most claims. What exactly is being claimed, and is it a disease claim in disguise? Was it tested in humans, at a dose a human would take? Compared against what? And who profits if you believe it?
