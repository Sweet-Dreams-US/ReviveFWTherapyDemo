The supplement aisle makes two quiet promises: that more is better, and that whatever you are missing, a capsule will cover it. Both are half true at best.

The part almost nobody explains is that vitamins do not all behave the same way once they are inside you. Some get stored. Some get flushed. That single difference decides which ones carry a real risk of overdoing it, which ones your body simply discards, and which ones are worth a blood test instead of a guess.

## The one split that explains most of the shelf

There are thirteen vitamins, and they sort into two camps based on what they dissolve in.

**Fat-soluble vitamins — A, D, E, and K** — ride along with the fat in your meal. They are absorbed into the lymph inside chylomicrons, the transport particles your gut builds for dietary fat, and then stored in your liver and fat tissue. Storage is the key word. What you take today is still partly there next month.

**Water-soluble vitamins — the eight B vitamins and vitamin C** — dissolve in blood plasma. Your kidneys filter the excess and most of it leaves in urine. Your body holds only small working pools, which is why intake matters more regularly.

That storage difference is why the toxicity risk sits almost entirely on the fat-soluble side. Preformed vitamin A, the retinol form found in liver and in some supplements, is the classic example — chronic high intakes are associated with liver problems and bone effects, and high intakes in pregnancy carry known risks. Vitamin D taken at very high supplemental doses over months can push blood calcium too high, which is a genuine clinical problem, not a theoretical one.

### What "you just pee it out" gets wrong

The flip side gets oversimplified. Water-soluble does not mean unlimited. Vitamin B6 taken at high doses over long periods is associated with nerve damage in the hands and feet, which is exactly why B6 has an established upper limit. Niacin at pharmacologic doses causes flushing intense enough that people think they are having a reaction.

The Dietary Reference Intakes include a value called the Tolerable Upper Intake Level, or UL. It is not a target. It is the point above which the risk of harm starts to climb, and for most nutrients it applies to what comes from supplements and fortified foods rather than what comes from a plate of food. It is very hard to overdo vitamins by eating. It is not hard at all with a pill organizer.

## What the B vitamins actually do, and why they don't give you energy

Walk past any cooler and you will find a drink advertising an enormous percentage of your daily B vitamins alongside the word energy. The chemistry does not support the implication.

B vitamins are **coenzymes** — helper molecules that enzymes need in order to run a reaction. Thiamin is required for the enzyme complex that moves pyruvate into the mitochondria. Riboflavin becomes FAD and niacin becomes NAD, the two electron carriers that shuttle energy through the whole system. Pantothenic acid becomes coenzyme A. Vitamin B6 becomes pyridoxal phosphate, the workhorse of amino acid metabolism. Biotin runs the carboxylase enzymes.

They are the tools. They are not the fuel. B vitamins contain no calories, and adding more of a coenzyme to an enzyme that already has enough does not make the reaction go faster — enzymes saturate. What you feel from an energy drink is caffeine and sugar.

Where B vitamins matter enormously is at the other end. Thiamin deficiency produces beriberi. Niacin deficiency produces pellagra. These are severe, and correcting them changes a person's life within days. The lesson of the deficiency-disease era is that these nutrients are non-negotiable, not that more is better once you have enough.

## Vitamin C, folate, and B12: the three worth understanding properly

**Vitamin C** is a cofactor for the enzymes that hydroxylate proline and lysine during collagen assembly. Without it, collagen comes out structurally weak, which is why scurvy shows up as bleeding gums, poor wound healing, and connective tissue that falls apart. Vitamin C also converts plant-source iron into the form your gut absorbs more readily, which matters in the next chapter of this story.

**Folate and B12** are tied together in one-carbon metabolism, and the way they interact creates a real clinical trap. B12 is required to release folate from a trapped methyl form and to regenerate methionine from homocysteine. When B12 is low, folate piles up in a form the cell cannot use.

Here is the trap: a high folate intake can normalize the blood picture — the large, immature red blood cells that would otherwise flag a problem — while an underlying B12 deficiency keeps quietly damaging nerves. The blood test looks better while the neurology gets worse.

**B12 absorption** is one of the most elaborate processes in nutrition. Stomach acid frees it from food protein. A binding protein escorts it through the stomach. Pancreatic enzymes hand it off to intrinsic factor, made by cells in your stomach lining. Only that complex is absorbed, and only in the last stretch of your small intestine.

Break any link and intake stops mattering. Stomach lining changes with age, long-term acid-suppressing medication, metformin, gastric or intestinal surgery, inflammatory bowel disease, and a fully plant-based diet without a supplement can all produce low B12 in someone eating what looks like enough.

This is a physician conversation, not a supplement-aisle decision. B12 status is a blood test, the underlying cause changes the treatment, and tingling or numbness in your hands and feet is a symptom to bring to a doctor rather than to a coach.

## Why the number on the label isn't the number in your body

Two foods can list the same amount of a vitamin and deliver very different amounts to your tissues. That gap is **bioavailability**, and the food matrix — everything else in the food — is a big part of it.

Beta-carotene from a raw carrot is locked inside plant cell walls. Cook the carrot and eat it with some fat, and considerably more gets through. Spinach is genuinely high in calcium, but oxalates bind most of it before you can absorb it. Folic acid, the synthetic form used in supplements and fortified grain, is absorbed more efficiently than the folate that occurs naturally in food.

Two words on packages get used interchangeably and are not the same. **Enrichment** puts back nutrients that processing removed — the B vitamins and iron added back into refined wheat flour. **Fortification** adds nutrients that were never meaningfully there — iodine in table salt, vitamin D in milk, folic acid in grain products. Both are public health engineering, and they are a large part of why the deficiency diseases that built this field are rare in the United States today.

## The part supplement marketing skips

For people already eating a reasonably varied diet, multivitamin trials have been persistently underwhelming for the outcomes people actually buy them for. That is not a fringe position. It is where the large randomized evidence has landed.

Vitamin D is the sharpest example of how this goes wrong. Observational research linked low blood levels to a startling range of conditions, and the supplement market responded accordingly. Large randomized trials handing out vitamin D have mostly not produced the benefits those associations implied. The most likely explanation is that a low blood level is partly a marker of other things — illness, less time outdoors, body composition — rather than purely a cause.

Northern Indiana winters are a legitimate reason to ask your doctor about testing your level. They are not a reason to start a high-dose regimen because a podcast host takes one.
