You sign a HIPAA acknowledgment at every medical office, and nobody has ever explained what you signed. Meanwhile you fill out a gym intake form listing your surgeries, your medications, and your injury history — and that form is very likely not covered by HIPAA at all.

Both of those facts are worth understanding, because one of them shapes your care and the other shapes who you hand your history to.

## What HIPAA actually covers

HIPAA's Privacy Rule and Security Rule apply to **covered entities**: health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers who transmit health information electronically in connection with certain standard transactions — which in practice means providers who bill insurance. It also reaches **business associates**, the vendors handling data on a covered entity's behalf, through contracts.

What it protects is **protected health information**, or PHI: individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by those entities.

The part people find surprising is how much sharing is permitted without any separate signature. A covered entity may use and disclose PHI for **treatment, payment, and health care operations**. Your surgeon sending records to your anesthesiologist, the billing office submitting a claim, the hospital reviewing charts for quality — all permitted. The form at the front desk is an acknowledgment that you received their privacy notice, not a consent form deciding those uses.

HIPAA also gives you rights, and the useful one is the **right of access**: you can obtain copies of your own records, generally within thirty days, for a reasonable cost-based fee.

## Consent versus authorization

For anything outside treatment, payment, and operations, you sign an **authorization** — the release of information form.

A valid authorization is specific. It names what information is released, who is releasing it, who receives it, the purpose, an expiration date, and your signature. That specificity is protection: "release my March lumbar MRI and associated radiology report to Dr. Nguyen for a second opinion" is a narrower door than "release my records."

You can revoke an authorization in writing, though not for what has already gone out. Read the scope before signing a broad one, especially forms from parties whose interests are not yours — an insurer, or an opposing attorney in an injury claim.

## A note is a legal document

Records are kept for years, and retention periods vary by state and provider type. That note from an urgent care visit in 2019 still exists.

If something in your record is wrong, HIPAA gives you the right to **request an amendment**. The provider can deny the request, but if they do, you may submit a statement of disagreement that gets included with the record going forward.

What you cannot do is delete anything. Corrections in a medical record append; they do not erase. That is deliberate — a record that could be quietly rewritten would be worthless as evidence of what was known and when. Which is also why an offhand line in a note can follow you for years, and why reading your own chart is worth the fifteen minutes.

## Your gym, your phone, and your photos

Here is the honest part, and it applies to us as much as anyone.

A fitness facility is generally **not** a HIPAA-covered entity. Neither are most wellness apps or wearables. So the intake form where you listed two surgeries and a medication is protected by that business's own policy, not by federal health privacy law. State consumer-protection and data-breach laws may still apply, but they are not HIPAA, and any gym telling you your information is "HIPAA protected" is either confused or improvising.

That does not mean the information is unprotected. It means the protection is a promise rather than a statute, so ask what the promise is:

- Who inside the facility can see my intake form?
- Where is it stored, and for how long after I cancel?
- Is any of it shared with third parties, including marketing platforms?
- Who has access to any assessment data or body composition results?

A facility that answers those questions immediately and specifically has thought about it. A facility that looks confused has not.

The same care applies to phones. Do not text a screenshot of your labs to a trainer — that is a medical record sitting in a consumer messaging app, and the person receiving it is not qualified to read it. Photography is a consent question too. Other people are in your frame, mid-lift, and some have real reasons not to appear online. Ask before you post someone.
