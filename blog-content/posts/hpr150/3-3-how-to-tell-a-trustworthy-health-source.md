Two accounts show up in your feed on the same morning. One says cold exposure after lifting blunts your gains. The other says it speeds recovery. Both say "studies show." Both are confident, both have large followings, and one is wrong.

You do not need a research degree to sort this out. You need four habits.

## Where to look when you want the actual research

**PubMed is free and public.** It indexes the biomedical literature, and anyone can search it.

Two things make it usable. First, **MeSH terms** — a controlled vocabulary the database uses to tag articles by subject, so your search finds papers whatever synonym the authors chose. Second, **filters**: systematic reviews and meta-analyses, humans only, last five or ten years. A wall of results becomes a readable dozen.

Start with reviews, not single studies. One trial is one result from one sample. A systematic review has already gathered everything on a question and weighed it, and the Cochrane library is the reference standard for that kind of synthesis.

When you open a paper, read the methods sentence in the abstract before the conclusion. How many people, who were they, how long, compared to what. If the answer is twelve trained young men over four weeks, you have a hypothesis, not an answer.

## Not every journal is a journal

**Open access** means the article is free to read. That is good, and unrelated to quality — open access journals include some of the most rigorous titles in medicine.

**Predatory journals** exploit that model, charging authors a fee and providing little or no real peer review. The tells are consistent: unsolicited flattering invitations to submit, publication promised within days, no identifiable editorial board, invented impact metrics, and a scope covering cardiology and civil engineering in one issue. Legitimate open access titles are indexed in the Directory of Open Access Journals; absence from any recognized index deserves suspicion.

**Preprints** are a third category: complete manuscripts posted publicly before peer review. Useful for speed, and explicitly provisional — findings may change or fail entirely. Treat a preprint as a serious draft, and be careful about anyone citing one as settled.

Papers also get retracted, and the retraction rarely travels as far as the claim. Retraction Watch tracks that.

## Grading a health website in ninety seconds

Six questions, roughly in order of importance:

1. **Who wrote it, and what are their credentials?** No named author is a finding in itself.
2. **When was it last updated?** A 2014 page on a fast-moving topic is a historical document.
3. **Does it cite primary sources you can actually click?** "Research shows" with no link is decoration.
4. **Is the site selling the thing it describes?** Not disqualifying, but it changes the weight.
5. **Does it name any uncertainty?** Real health writing includes limits. Content that has never met a caveat is advertising.
6. **Does the cited study match the claim?** This is where most failures are. Check that the study was in humans, measured the outcome claimed, and found an effect resembling the headline.

Government and academic sources are a reasonable anchor — MedlinePlus from the National Library of Medicine, agency sites, university health libraries — not because institutions are infallible, but because they correct themselves in public.

## What "evidence-based" is supposed to mean

The original definition, from the clinicians who built the concept, has three legs: the best available research evidence, clinical expertise, and the patient's values and circumstances. All three. It was never "a study exists."

Watch how the phrase gets abused:

- **"Evidence-based" stamped on a product** because one cell study found the ingredient does something in a dish.
- **"Clinically proven"**, which is not a regulated term and can mean almost anything.
- **A rodent study cited for a human claim.** Common in supplement marketing.
- **A study funded and run by the manufacturer**, presented without that disclosure.
- **"Studies show"** with no study named. A claim that cannot be traced to a source is not sourced, it is laundered — the professional version of that failure is plagiarism, which is why serious writing carries citations you can check.

The counter-question is short: which study, in whom, compared to what, and who paid? Most confident claims do not survive it.

## The uncomfortable part

Do all of this correctly and you will still often land on "probably," because much of exercise and recovery research runs on small samples over short timelines. Good sources also disagree, and a healthy field changes its mind — which looks like chaos from outside and is the system working.

Apply the same test to us. If a post here makes a claim without telling you where the evidence is thin, hold it to the standard above.
