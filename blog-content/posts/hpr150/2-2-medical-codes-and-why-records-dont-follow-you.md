You get referred to a specialist four miles from your primary care office. You arrive, and they hand you a clipboard asking for your surgeries, your medications, and the year of your last tetanus shot. Everything on that clipboard already exists in a computer down the road.

This is not incompetence. It is architecture, and understanding it tells you exactly which parts you can work around.

## Why your records do not travel

Electronic health records were built as billing and workflow systems for individual organizations, not as a shared library. Each hospital system runs its own installation — Epic and Oracle Health, formerly Cerner, dominate the large-system market — and each one is configured locally, with its own templates and its own internal vocabulary.

Systems do exchange data. Standards like HL7 and its newer version, FHIR, exist precisely so one record can be read by another, and federal information blocking rules now prohibit unreasonably withholding records. But "technically able to exchange" and "your allergy list shows up correctly in the new office's screen" are different achievements. Data that arrives often lands as a PDF attachment nobody opens rather than as structured fields the system can use.

The practical consequence: the most reliable carrier of your medical history between organizations is still you.

## What the codes on your bill mean

Two systems, two different questions.

**ICD-10-CM** codes answer *why*. They are diagnosis codes, maintained in the U.S. by federal health agencies, and there are tens of thousands of them. M54.5 territory is low back pain. They appear on your claim to justify why a service was necessary.

**CPT** codes answer *what was done*. Maintained by the American Medical Association, they describe procedures and services — an office visit of a given complexity, an injection, an X-ray view.

Codes are assigned by clinicians and trained coders working from documentation. Not by you, and not by a receptionist guessing. A code has to be supported by what is written in the note, which is a large part of why notes are written the way they are.

Here is the part people misread. A diagnosis code on a claim is a billing statement, not a verdict. If your doctor ordered a test to rule something out, a related code may appear on the paperwork simply to justify the test. Seeing an alarming term on an explanation of benefits does not mean you have been diagnosed with it. Ask before you spiral.

Codes also get entered wrong. Billing errors are common enough that reviewing a large bill line by line, and calling about anything that does not match what happened, is ordinary consumer behavior rather than paranoia.

## The abbreviations hospitals banned

The Joint Commission, which accredits U.S. hospitals, maintains a **Do Not Use list** of abbreviations prohibited in clinical documentation because they killed people.

The banned entries include *U* for unit, because a handwritten 10U was read as 100. *IU* for international unit, misread as IV. *QD* and *QOD* for daily and every other day, confused with each other. Trailing zeros — writing 1.0 mg, where a missed decimal reads as 10 mg. Missing leading zeros — writing .5 mg instead of 0.5 mg, read as 5 mg. And *MS*, *MSO4*, and *MgSO4*, which could mean morphine sulfate or magnesium sulfate.

Every item on that list is a decimal point or a single character standing between a correct dose and a tenfold overdose. Worth remembering the next time an abbreviation on your own paperwork looks ambiguous: if it is unclear to you, ask, because ambiguity is exactly the failure mode the list exists to prevent.

## The one page worth carrying

Weed's problem-oriented record had a **problem list** at the front — a running index of active issues so any clinician could orient in thirty seconds. The modern electronic equivalent is the continuity of care document, a structured summary meant to travel between organizations.

You can build your own version, and it will outperform any system currently in place. One page, updated twice a year:

- Active conditions, with the year each was diagnosed
- Surgeries and hospitalizations, with dates
- Every medication and supplement, with doses
- Allergies and what the reaction actually was
- Key test results and when they were done
- Family history in one line each

Take a photo of it. When the clipboard appears, you are answering from a document instead of from memory at a moment when you are already anxious.
