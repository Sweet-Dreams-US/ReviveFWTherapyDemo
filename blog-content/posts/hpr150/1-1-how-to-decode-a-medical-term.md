Your doctor says "lateral epicondylitis," and you nod, because the alternative is admitting you have no idea what just happened to your arm. Then you get to the parking lot and type it into your phone.

Here is what nobody tells you: medical words are not arbitrary. They are assembled from parts, and there are only a few hundred parts in common use. Learn the frequent ones and most terms stop being vocabulary you have to memorize and start being short sentences you can read.

## Almost every medical term is two to four pieces

A term usually has a **root** (the body part or subject), sometimes a **prefix** in front of it (where, how many, or whether), and a **suffix** at the end (what is happening, or what someone plans to do about it).

Common roots: *cardi-* heart, *oste-* bone, *arthr-* joint, *my-* muscle, *neur-* nerve, *derm-* skin, *gastr-* stomach.

Between two parts you will often find a **combining vowel**, almost always an *o*, there purely to make the word pronounceable. *Oste* + *o* + *arthr* + *itis* becomes osteoarthritis. The vowel drops out when the next part already starts with one, which is why it is gastritis and not gastro-itis.

Read the term backwards. Take the suffix first, then the front, then the middle. *Electro-cardio-gram* — a record, of the heart, electrical. A printout of your heart's electrical activity.

Now the elbow: *epi-* (upon) + *condyle* (the knuckle-shaped bump at the end of a bone) + *-itis* (inflammation) equals irritation at the bump on the outside of your elbow. Tennis elbow.

## Why the plurals look wrong

Because they are Greek and Latin, not English. One vertebra, two vertebrae. One bursa, two bursae. One thrombus, two thrombi. One meniscus, two menisci. One diagnosis, two diagnoses. One phalanx, several phalanges.

The same history explains why two totally different words point at one organ. Your kidney is *ren-* in Latin and *nephr-* in Greek, so you have a renal artery but you see a nephrologist. That is not sloppiness. Anatomy came into English largely through Latin, while disease and procedure names came largely through Greek, and both stuck.

## Prefixes tell you where, how many, or whether

**Position and direction:** *sub-* below, *supra-* above, *inter-* between (intervertebral, between the vertebrae), *intra-* within (intramuscular, into the muscle), *peri-* around (pericardium, the sac around the heart), *retro-* behind, *ante-* before, *post-* after.

**Number and amount:** *uni-* one, *bi-* two, *hemi-* half (hemiplegia, weakness on half the body), *poly-* many, *oligo-* few.

**Negation and degree:** *a-* or *an-* without, *dys-* difficult or disordered, *hyper-* above normal, *hypo-* below normal, *brady-* slow, *tachy-* fast.

Put it together: hypoglycemia is *hypo* (low) plus *glyc* (sugar) plus *-emia* (a blood condition). Low blood sugar. Three parts, one plain-English sentence.

## Suffixes tell you what happened, or what they intend to do

**Conditions:** *-itis* inflammation, *-osis* an abnormal condition (often degenerative), *-pathy* disease of, *-algia* pain, *-emia* blood condition, *-oma* mass or tumor, *-megaly* enlargement, *-sclerosis* hardening, *-penia* deficiency.

**Procedures:** *-ectomy* removal, *-otomy* cutting into, *-ostomy* creating an opening, *-plasty* surgical repair, *-scopy* looking inside with a camera, *-graphy* the process of imaging, *-gram* the image itself, *-centesis* puncturing to withdraw fluid.

So "arthroscopic partial meniscectomy" decodes to: they looked inside the joint with a camera and removed part of the meniscus. That is a sentence, not an incantation.

## Where the decoding stops working

Two honest limits.

First, eponyms decode to nothing. Achilles tendon, Bell's palsy, Osgood-Schlatter — these are named after people or myths, and no amount of Greek helps.

Second, the parts can quietly mislead. For decades, stubborn tendon pain was called "tendinitis," implying inflammation. When researchers actually biopsied those tendons, they often found degenerative change rather than classic inflammation, which is why you now see "tendinopathy" and "tendinosis" in reports. Same tendon, different suffix, meaningfully different thinking about how it gets better.

And the largest limit of all: knowing what a word means is not knowing what it means for you. Word parts describe a category. Whether that category explains your pain, how serious it is, and what to do next is a clinical judgment that belongs to your physician. Decoding buys you better questions. It does not buy you a diagnosis.

## The Revive Tip

Next time you leave an appointment holding a word you do not know, type it into your phone before you reach the car, spelled the way you heard it. Break it apart on the drive home. Then bring it in. Tell a coach the word and, more importantly, the restrictions you were given — "nothing overhead for six weeks" changes a program far more than the diagnosis does.
