The portal notification lands at 6:40 on a Tuesday: "Multilevel degenerative disc disease. Mild neural foraminal narrowing at L4-L5." Your appointment to talk it through is nine days out. You have nine days and a phone, which is a bad combination.

You can learn enough of this language to read a report as a description rather than a sentence. Here is the vocabulary that shows up most often in the reports members actually bring in — bones and joints, heart and vessels, lungs, and nerves.

## The words on a musculoskeletal report

Roots first: *oste-* bone, *arthr-* joint, *chondr-* cartilage, *my-* muscle, *tendin-* tendon, *spondyl-* vertebra.

Then the descriptors. **Stenosis** is narrowing. A **foramen** is an opening a nerve root passes through, so "neural foraminal narrowing" means that opening measures smaller than average. **Effusion** is fluid inside a joint. **Edema** is swelling within tissue. **Spondylolisthesis** is one vertebra sitting forward of the one below it. **Osteophyte** is a bone spur.

Suffixes finish the sentence: *-itis* inflammation, *-osis* an abnormal or degenerative condition, *-pathy* disease of, *-algia* pain, *-malacia* softening.

### Why "degenerative changes" appears in people with no pain

A radiologist's job is to describe what is on the image. What is on the image includes ordinary aging.

Systematic reviews of MRI scans taken from volunteers with no back pain at all find disc degeneration, disc bulges, and other "degenerative" findings in a large share of them — and the share climbs steadily with each decade of age. Similar patterns turn up in shoulders and knees. These are common features of lived-in bodies, not evidence that something has gone wrong.

That leads to the single most useful idea in this post. A finding is a fact about an image. Whether that finding explains your symptoms is a clinical judgment requiring your history, your physical exam, and your physician. The report cannot make that call, and neither can you or a coach.

The words *mild*, *moderate*, and *severe* deserve the same caution. They grade what the picture looks like, not how much your life is affected.

## Heart and circulation words

*Cardi-* heart, *angi-* and *vas-* vessel, *hem-* and *hemat-* blood, *thromb-* clot, *lymph-* lymphatic fluid.

**Tachycardia** is a fast heart rate, **bradycardia** a slow one, **arrhythmia** an irregular one. **Ischemia** means tissue is not getting enough blood flow. **Infarction** means tissue died because of it. **Atherosclerosis** is plaque buildup and stiffening in arteries. **Hypertension** is elevated blood pressure. **Lymphedema** is swelling from lymph fluid that is not draining well.

The imaging suffixes repeat: an **echocardiogram** is an ultrasound picture of the heart, **angiography** is imaging of vessels, and an **electrocardiogram** — ECG, or EKG from the German spelling — is a record of the heart's electrical activity.

## Breathing words

*Pulmon-* and *pneum-* refer to lungs and air, *bronch-* to the airways, and the suffix *-pnea* to breathing itself.

**Dyspnea** is difficult breathing, the clinical term for shortness of breath. **Apnea** is absence of breathing, as in sleep apnea. **Tachypnea** is rapid breathing. **Hypoxemia** is low oxygen in the blood. **Spirometry** is a measurement of how much and how fast you can move air; **bronchoscopy** is looking inside the airways with a scope.

One you may meet at the gym: **exercise-induced bronchoconstriction**, meaning airways that narrow during hard efforts. Fort Wayne's cold, dry January air is a commonly reported trigger for people who have it. If hard breathing turns into wheezing or a cough that lingers well after you stop, that is a physician conversation, not a coaching adjustment.

## Nerve and sensation words

*Neur-* nerve, *radicul-* nerve root, *encephal-* brain, *myel-* spinal cord or bone marrow depending on context, *-esthesia* sensation, *-plegia* paralysis, *-paresis* weakness.

**Radiculopathy** is a problem at a nerve root — what most people call a pinched nerve. **Paresthesia** is the pins-and-needles feeling. **Neuropathy** is nerve disease, often the numb or burning-feet variety. For the senses, *ophthalm-* and *-opia* cover eyes, *ot-* covers ears, and tinnitus is simply Latin for ringing.

## What a report is honestly for

Imaging reports are written by one clinician for another clinician. The "Impression" at the bottom is the radiologist's summary of the images, and even that is not a diagnosis. Your physician combines it with everything a scanner cannot see.

So read yours to build questions, not conclusions. Do not start, stop, or change anything based on a report you decoded alone at 6:40 on a Tuesday.
