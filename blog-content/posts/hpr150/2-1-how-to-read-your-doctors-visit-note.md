Somewhere in your patient portal is a note your doctor wrote about you fifteen minutes after you left. Since federal information blocking rules took effect in 2021, you have had the right to read it. Most people open one, hit a line like "patient denies chest pain, appears well-nourished," feel vaguely insulted, and never open another.

That reaction is a translation problem. Clinical notes are written in a compressed, deliberately flat format for other clinicians. Once you know the format, your own chart becomes one of the more useful documents you own.

## The four blocks of a SOAP note

Most notes follow a structure a physician named Lawrence Weed popularized in the 1960s, when he argued that medical records should be organized around problems rather than scattered through a diary. It goes by its initials.

**S — Subjective.** What you reported. Your symptoms, your history, in your words or a summary of them. This is the section where "patient reports" and "patient denies" live.

**O — Objective.** What was measured or observed. Vitals, exam findings, lab and imaging results. Facts that would look the same to any competent observer.

**A — Assessment.** What the clinician thinks is going on, and how confident they are. This is the interpretation section, and it is the one worth reading twice.

**P — Plan.** What happens next. Tests, prescriptions, referrals, follow-up interval, and what you were told to do.

If you only read one section, read the Plan. It is the part with your name on the homework.

## The other formats you will run into

Not everyone uses SOAP. **DAP notes** collapse it to Data, Assessment, Plan and are common in behavioral health. **Narrative notes** read as paragraphs and show up when a situation is too complicated for boxes. **Flow sheets** are grids — vitals every four hours, or a physical therapy log tracking the same six measures across twelve visits — and they exist because a trend over time is the actual information.

An emergency department note, a physical therapy note, and an annual physical note all look different because they answer different questions. None of them are trying to tell your life story.

## Why the wording sounds so cold

Two rules drive the tone, and both are in your favor.

The first is that documentation separates observation from inference. "Patient winced and withdrew the arm during shoulder abduction" is an observation. "Patient is in a lot of pain" is an inference. "Patient is exaggerating" is an inference plus a judgment, and it has no business in a record. Good documentation stays as close to the first sentence as possible, which is why notes read like a lab report rather than a description of a person.

The second is that "denies" is a documentation verb, not an accusation. When a note says you denied fever, it means you were asked about fever and said no. Recording the absence of a symptom is genuinely important — it shows what was considered and ruled out. Nobody is calling you a liar.

## What belongs in a gym's record, and what does not

Revive is not a clinic, and the record we keep on you should look nothing like a medical chart.

What belongs: what you did, how much, how long, and how you responded. Loads, sets, session durations, recovery modalities used, what you reported afterward. Restrictions your physician gave you, recorded as you relayed them. Injuries you mentioned, described in your words.

What does not belong: a diagnosis assigned by anyone here, an interpretation of your labs or imaging, an opinion about your medication, or a coach's theory about why your back hurts. Those cross a professional line, and a facility that writes them down is telling you something about its judgment.

The honest version of this is worth saying plainly. A training log is a record of training. It is not evidence about your health, it does not travel to your doctor, and it should never be treated as a second opinion.

## Where notes go wrong

Notes are written under time pressure by tired people, and they contain errors. Templates auto-fill sections nobody edited. Copy-forward carries a paragraph from a visit two years ago into today's note, which is how "current smoker" follows someone around for a decade after they quit.

If you find something inaccurate in your chart, that is worth flagging — and there is a formal process for it, which is the subject of a later post. For now: read your notes precisely because nobody else is proofreading them.
