Your results post to the portal at 9:15 on a Friday night. Three values are flagged in red. Your appointment is Tuesday. By Saturday morning you have read enough of the internet to be genuinely frightened about something that may turn out to be nothing.

Nothing in this post interprets your results — that is your physician's job, and it requires everything a lab report does not contain. What it can do is explain what those numbers are, so the weekend is less awful.

## What a reference range actually is

A reference range is not a boundary between healthy and sick. It is usually built by measuring a population of reference subjects and taking the middle 95% of their results.

Read that again, because the consequence is the whole point: **by construction, about one in twenty perfectly healthy people falls outside any given range.** The range was defined that way on purpose.

Now stack tests. A standard panel measures a dozen or more analytes. If those results were independent, the odds of at least one falling outside its range would be close to a coin flip for a completely healthy person. Panels flag things. That is what panels do.

A few more facts that lower the temperature:

- Ranges vary by laboratory, because they depend on the instrument and assay. Comparing your value to a range you found online is using the wrong ruler.
- Many ranges vary by age and sex, and some shift with time of day, food, hydration, and recent hard training. A brutal week can move certain markers, which is a real reason to mention your training when results look odd.
- The red flag is generated automatically by software. No human judged your specific case before it appeared on your screen.
- A trend across three tests carries more information than a single value, which is why your physician may repeat a test rather than act on one number.

Which leads to the rule for the weekend: do not start, stop, or change anything — medication, supplement, diet, training — based on a flagged value you read alone. Write down your questions and bring the whole report to the person qualified to read it in context. A coach offering to interpret your bloodwork is a coach operating well outside their lane.

## "Cuts your risk in half" — of what?

The second numeracy trap is risk, and it shows up everywhere from drug ads to supplement labels.

Use round made-up numbers to see the shape. Suppose out of 1,000 people, 2 develop a condition over ten years. An intervention brings that to 1 in 1,000.

Both of these statements are true:

- It reduced the risk by 50%. That is the **relative risk reduction**.
- It reduced the risk by 0.1 percentage points. That is the **absolute risk reduction**.

The first sounds enormous. The second tells you what to expect. Marketing quotes the first almost every time, and it is not technically lying — which is why relative risk quoted without absolute risk should make you suspicious.

A third framing beats both: **number needed to treat**, or how many people must take something for one to benefit. In this made-up example, 1,000. Sometimes that number is small and the intervention is clearly worth it. Sometimes it is enormous and you are being sold a rounding error.

## Numbers you can picture

Research on risk communication has shown repeatedly that people reason better with **natural frequencies** than with percentages. "8 out of every 1,000 people" lands more accurately than "0.8%," and that holds even among trained professionals.

**Icon arrays** work the same way — a grid of 100 figures with the affected ones shaded — because they show the denominator instead of hiding it.

So when you hear any risk claim, ask three questions: out of how many people, over how long, and compared to what? A claim missing any of those is not a claim yet.

## When "we don't know" is the right answer

Some questions are genuinely unresolved, and honest communication says so.

That is what a confidence interval is for. It expresses a range of values the data are reasonably compatible with, and a wide one means the study could not pin the answer down. A single tidy number with no interval, and no discussion of what would change the conclusion, has been dressed up for presentation.

A physician who says "we don't know, and here is how we would find out" is giving you better information than one who sounds certain about everything. The same standard applies to anyone in a gym.
