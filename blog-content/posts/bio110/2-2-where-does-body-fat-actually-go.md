You lost twelve pounds. Twelve pounds of physical material used to be attached to you and now is not. It did not evaporate into nothing, and it did not convert into pure energy — matter does not work that way at the scale of a human body.

So where did it go. Most people, including plenty of trainers, guess sweat. The real answer is stranger and more useful, and it starts with a rule you learned once and probably never applied to yourself.

## Matter has to go somewhere

Chemical equations balance. Every atom that goes into a reaction comes out of it somewhere, in some arrangement. That is conservation of mass, and it is not a guideline.

Body fat is stored as triglyceride, a molecule built almost entirely of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. To release its energy, your cells combine it with oxygen and take it apart in stages. What comes out the other side is carbon dioxide and water. There are no other products.

The carbon dioxide leaves through your lungs. You exhale it, breath after breath, and it accounts for most of the mass. The water leaves in urine, sweat, breath, and tears, in no particular order.

So the honest sentence is that you breathe out the large majority of the fat you lose. Not because breathing is a fat-loss technique — you cannot force the process by breathing harder — but because the lungs are the exit door for carbon.

## What this rules out

Once you accept the accounting, several popular claims collapse on inspection.

Sweat is water and dissolved salts. Losing four pounds in a sauna is four pounds of water, and it returns with your next glass. A wrap, a suit, or a hot room cannot change what fat is made of or where its atoms can exit.

Fat also does not turn into muscle. They are different molecules built from different atoms, and there is no reaction that converts one into the other. You can lose one and build the other in the same months, which is what people are describing when they say it, but nothing is being transmuted.

And spot reduction fails for the same reason. The atoms leave through your bloodstream and your lungs, not through the skin above the muscle you just trained. Where your body draws from is not something a crunch gets to vote on.

## Why 500 milligrams is rarely 500 milligrams

Here is where naming conventions cost people money. A mineral in a supplement is never a bare mineral — it is bonded to something, and the number on the label may describe the whole compound.

Magnesium oxide is roughly 60 percent magnesium by mass. Magnesium citrate is closer to 16 percent, because citrate is a much larger partner. So 500 milligrams of one is not 500 milligrams of the other, and neither is 500 milligrams of magnesium. Calcium works the same way: carbonate carries more calcium per gram than citrate does.

That does not automatically make oxide the better buy, because absorption differs too, and the poorly absorbed forms often carry more elemental mineral on paper. The point is narrower and more practical. Look for the word elemental, and if the label does not distinguish, you cannot tell what you bought.

## The scarcest ingredient sets the ceiling

In chemistry, the limiting reagent is the ingredient that runs out first and caps how much product you can make. Everything else left over is surplus, no matter how much of it you had.

Protein synthesis behaves this way. Building a protein requires all the necessary amino acids present at once, so the one in shortest supply sets the pace. That is the real logic behind protein quality, and it is why doubling a supplement that supplies only one or two amino acids does not build anything if a different one is the bottleneck.

Chemists also distinguish theoretical yield from actual yield, and biology never hits the theoretical figure. Some of what you eat is not absorbed, some is oxidized for fuel instead of built into tissue, and some is lost in the machinery. This is the honest limit worth carrying: the mass balance is real and unbreakable, but no calculation on paper predicts exactly what your particular body does with a meal. The arithmetic sets the boundaries. It does not fill in the details.
