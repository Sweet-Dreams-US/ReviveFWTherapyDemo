You are eight miles into a humid Saturday, you drink the electrolyte mix you brought, and twenty minutes later your stomach is sloshing and your mouth is drier than before. You did everything the label suggested.

The label was not wrong about its ingredients. It was quiet about the thing that decides whether a drink helps or hurts, which is not what is in it but how concentrated it is.

## Concentration is a ratio, not an amount

Every way of expressing concentration is some version of how much stuff per how much liquid. Molarity is moles of substance per liter. Percent composition is parts per hundred. Parts per million shows up for contaminants and trace additives. Milliequivalents per liter, used for electrolytes on blood work, counts electrical charge rather than particles.

The reason this matters to you is that a fixed amount of sodium in a small volume behaves completely differently from the same sodium in a large volume. The amount on the label is only half the information.

It also explains a result that confuses people. Low blood sodium usually does not mean you are short on salt. It means there is too much water relative to the sodium you have. Same numerator, different denominator, opposite conclusion.

## Water follows salt

Cell membranes let water through much more easily than they let dissolved particles through. So when the concentration on one side differs from the other, water moves toward the more concentrated side to even things out. That is osmosis, and the pressure it can generate is real and substantial.

Tonicity describes the comparison. A fluid matching your blood is isotonic, and cells sitting in it stay as they are. A more dilute fluid is hypotonic, and water moves into cells, swelling them. A more concentrated fluid is hypertonic, and water is drawn out of cells, shrinking them.

This is why intravenous fluids in a hospital are formulated to match blood concentration rather than being plain water. It is also why a salt tablet swallowed without enough fluid can work against you: you have raised the concentration in your gut and given water a reason to move in that direction.

## Why the drink sits in your stomach

A drink has to leave your stomach before it can be absorbed, and how fast that happens depends heavily on concentration.

A strongly hypertonic drink — a concentrated mix, a gel taken dry, soda — empties slowly, and it pulls water from your body into your gut on the way. That is where the sloshing, the cramping, and the feeling of being simultaneously full and thirsty come from. A more dilute solution moves through faster and reaches your bloodstream sooner.

That is the actual logic behind sports drinks being formulated around a relatively low carbohydrate concentration. It is not a flavor decision. Push the concentration up and absorption gets slower rather than faster.

Two related points. Dissolution rate is not the same thing as solubility — a powder dissolves faster than a tablet and warm water dissolves faster than cold, but the total that can dissolve and the dose you actually get are unchanged. And "rapid absorption" on a label is mostly marketing, because the rate-limiting step is usually your stomach, not the molecule.

## What your kidneys are actually doing

Your kidneys work by filtration followed by selective reabsorption. Blood is pushed through a filter under pressure, small molecules and water go through indiscriminately, and then your body reclaims what it wants to keep and lets the rest go. Dialysis is a machine version of the same idea, using a membrane and a concentration gradient to pull waste out of blood.

That mechanism is worth knowing because it kills the detox category outright. Removing waste requires a filter, a gradient, and an exit route. Your kidneys and liver have all three. A drink, a sauna, a wrap, and a foot bath do not. Sweat is not a meaningful excretion route for anything except water and salt, and no product enhances a filtration system that is already running continuously.

The honest limit on hydration is that it is genuinely individual. Sweat rates and sweat sodium vary enormously between people and between days, there is no practical way to measure yours precisely outside a lab, and overdrinking plain water during long efforts causes exercise-associated hyponatremia, which is more dangerous than mild dehydration. Drink to thirst, salt your food, and be suspicious of any protocol with exact numbers in it.

## The Revive Tip

Test your drink on a long training day, never on a race day. Fort Wayne summer mornings are humid enough that a mix which felt fine in April can turn on you in July.

Saturday Run Club is the right lab for this. Bring what you plan to use, dilute it more than the label says, and see what your stomach does over the back half of the run.
