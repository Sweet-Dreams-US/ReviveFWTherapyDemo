Somebody in your gym got a DEXA scan and now knows their body fat to one decimal place. You are curious, and also slightly uneasy, because the word radiation is attached to it and nobody explained that part.

Fair question, and the answer is more interesting than a yes or no. Not all radiation exposure is the same thing, and the machines used in medicine differ enormously in what they put into you.

## What radioactive actually means

Some atoms have an unstable nucleus. To settle down, they throw something off — that is radioactive decay, and it comes in a few flavors.

Alpha particles are heavy and stop at a sheet of paper or your skin, which makes them harmless outside the body and a real problem inside it. Beta particles are electrons, faster and more penetrating. Gamma rays are pure energy, and they pass through tissue, which is exactly why they are useful for imaging.

Half-life is the other half of the story. It is the time it takes for half of a sample to decay, and it is a fixed property of that isotope. The tracers used in medical imaging are deliberately chosen with short half-lives — hours, not years — so that most of the activity is gone before you have finished dinner.

## What the machines in a clinic are actually doing

A DEXA scan — dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry — has no radioactive source in it at all. It is an X-ray tube that fires two energy levels through you and compares how much each one is absorbed. Bone, fat, and lean tissue absorb differently, and that difference is the measurement. When you switch the machine off, the radiation stops.

A bone scan is different. There, a short-lived tracer is injected and travels to areas of active bone turnover, and the camera detects what the tracer emits from inside you. A PET scan usually uses a glucose lookalike carrying an unstable fluorine atom. It goes where glucose goes, which is why metabolically busy tissue lights up.

Those distinctions matter for dose. A DEXA scan is on the low end of medical imaging, delivering a small fraction of the natural background radiation you absorb over a year from soil, building materials, cosmic rays, and the potassium in your own body. A CT scan is in a completely different category. Grouping them together as radiation is how people end up either needlessly frightened or carelessly casual.

## The measurement nobody offers you

There is a gold-standard tool for measuring how much energy a person actually burns in daily life, and it uses isotopes without any radioactivity at all.

Doubly labeled water uses stable isotopes — a heavier hydrogen and a heavier oxygen. You drink it, then researchers track how fast each label disappears from your body. Hydrogen leaves as water. Oxygen leaves as both water and carbon dioxide. The gap between those two rates tells you how much carbon dioxide you produced, and that tells you how much fuel you burned, over a week or two of ordinary life.

It is expensive and it requires a lab, which is why no wearable is doing this. When you see a study on real-world energy expenditure that you can take seriously, it was often measured this way.

## Dose, and the principle behind every imaging room

Radiation dose is measured in sieverts, and medical exposures are usually described in millisieverts — thousandths of one. The framework that governs how those exposures are handled is ALARA: as low as reasonably achievable. It is why the technologist steps behind a wall while you stay put. Their exposure adds up across hundreds of patients a week; yours does not.

Now the honest limit, and it has nothing to do with safety. DEXA was developed and validated for bone density. Body composition is a secondary use, and repeat scans on the same person vary with hydration, with a recent meal, and with how you were positioned on the table. Numbers from two different machines are not directly comparable at all. A single scan is a snapshot with real error bars, not a truth you can chase to a tenth of a percent.

Whether you should get any scan, including this one, is a conversation with a physician who knows your history. It is not a purchase decision.
