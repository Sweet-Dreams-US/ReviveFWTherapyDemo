Look at the back of a multivitamin. Calcium is listed in hundreds of milligrams. Selenium and iodine are in micrograms — millionths of a gram. Both are labeled essential, and one is being dosed a thousand times smaller than the other.

That is not a hedge or a cost decision. It comes straight out of what those elements do once they are inside you, and it is the single most useful thing to understand before you buy anything off the mineral shelf.

## Six elements are basically all of you

Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen make up the large majority of your body mass. Add calcium and phosphorus, mostly locked into your skeleton, and you are at roughly 99 percent of the whole person. Everything else — every mineral that gets its own marketing campaign — divides up the remaining sliver.

What makes an element that element is the number of protons in its nucleus. Change the neutron count and you get an isotope: same element, same chemistry, different mass. That distinction matters more than it sounds, and it is how researchers track energy use and metabolism without changing how a molecule behaves.

The four big ones are the elements that build structures. You need them by the kilogram because they are the bricks.

## Why sodium and potassium behave alike

The periodic table is not a list. It is a map of how atoms behave, and the columns are the point.

Sodium and potassium sit in the same column, each with a single electron in its outermost shell that it gives up easily. Both become ions carrying one positive charge, which is why your nerves and muscles can build their entire electrical system around moving them across membranes.

Calcium and magnesium sit in the next column over. Both give up two electrons and carry two positive charges, which is why they compete for some of the same transport routes and binding sites and why megadosing one can crowd the other.

On the far side of the table, oxygen and chlorine do the opposite — they pull electrons hard. That tendency, electronegativity, is why oxygen ends up at the end of nearly every energy-releasing reaction in your cells, and why chloride tags along with sodium almost everywhere it goes.

## Why the trace list is dosed in millionths of a gram

Here is the split that explains the label. Some elements are structural. Others are catalytic.

Iron sits at the center of hemoglobin, holding the oxygen. Zinc sits at the working site of hundreds of enzymes. Selenium is built into an antioxidant enzyme. Iodine is a structural piece of thyroid hormone. Cobalt is buried inside vitamin B12.

In each case you need one atom, in one place, in a molecule that gets reused over and over. A catalyst is not consumed by the reaction it enables. So a pinch is genuinely enough, and the gap between not enough and too much is narrow. High-dose zinc taken for months interferes with copper absorption and can create a copper problem you did not have. Selenium has one of the tighter upper limits in the whole set.

More of a catalyst does not mean faster. It means you have exceeded what the system can place, and the excess has to go somewhere.

## Why toxic metals are dangerous in the first place

Poisonous metals are not dangerous because they are exotic. They are dangerous because they are convincing impostors.

Lead behaves enough like calcium that your body's calcium machinery handles it and files some of it into bone. Cadmium resembles zinc closely enough to slot into places zinc belongs. Mercury latches onto the sulfur-containing groups that hold proteins in their working shape. In each case the chemistry that makes a useful mineral useful is the same chemistry that lets a lookalike take its seat.

That is also the honest limit on this whole topic. Blood levels for many trace minerals are poor indicators of what is actually stored in tissue, and hair mineral analysis and direct-to-consumer "mineral deficiency" panels are not reliable ways to find out. Iron and iodine status can be assessed properly through a physician. For most of the rest, a varied diet does the job, and a supplement bought on a hunch is more likely to create an imbalance than to correct one.
