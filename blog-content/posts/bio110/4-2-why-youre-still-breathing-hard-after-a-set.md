You rack the bar and stop moving. Two minutes later you are still breathing like the set is happening. Nothing is wrong with you. Your lungs are the fastest acid-removal tool you own, and they are still working.

## The burn is not lactic acid

Push a set past the point where oxygen delivery keeps up and fast glycolysis takes over, producing lactate along with hydrogen ions. Lactate has spent decades taking blame for something it does not do. It is a fuel, shuttled between muscle fibers, taken up by the heart, converted back toward glucose in the liver.

The hydrogen ions are the acid part. Rising hydrogen ion concentration inside the working muscle is one of the things you feel; accumulating inorganic phosphate is another. Those metabolites stimulate small sensory nerve endings, and what comes back is the burn.

Worth being honest, because the correction gets oversold in its own direction: the exact recipe behind that sensation is not fully settled. And next-day soreness is different entirely. It is not lactic acid — lactate is long gone before you go to bed. The leading explanations involve mechanical disruption of muscle fibers and the inflammation that follows, and the mechanism is still argued about after decades of study.

## What a buffer is, and why blood pH barely moves

A buffer is a weak acid and its conjugate base living together in the same solution. Add acid and the base soaks up the extra protons; add base and the acid releases some. The pair resists change in both directions.

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation puts a number on this, and its useful lesson is that the pH of a buffered solution depends on the ratio of base to acid, not the absolute amount of either. That is why you can pour acid in and watch the pH barely move at first, then give way quickly once the ratio gets extreme.

How much acid a buffer absorbs before that happens is its buffer capacity. It is finite, and hard training is you spending it.

## Why breathing is a pH control, not just an oxygen control

The main buffer in your blood is bicarbonate: carbon dioxide plus water makes carbonic acid, which comes apart into a hydrogen ion and a bicarbonate ion. An enzyme called carbonic anhydrase runs the first step fast enough to matter.

What makes this buffer unusual is that one component is a gas you can exhale. Breathe harder, remove more carbon dioxide, and the chain gets pulled backward — hydrogen ions consumed rebuilding the carbon dioxide you just blew off. You lowered the acidity of your blood by breathing.

So those two minutes of hard breathing are not purely oxygen hunger. Part of it is your body exporting acid through your lungs, which it can do in seconds. Your kidneys handle the slower version, excreting hydrogen ions and regenerating bicarbonate over hours and days.

Two other buffers deserve mention. Hemoglobin is one, elegantly so: as it releases oxygen in a working muscle it becomes better at binding hydrogen ions, picking up acid exactly where acid is produced. Phosphate and protein side chains do most of the buffering inside cells, where bicarbonate is less useful.

## The four ways this actually goes wrong

Real acid-base disturbances come in two origins and two directions. Respiratory means the problem is carbon dioxide. Metabolic means it is anything else.

Respiratory acidosis is failing to clear carbon dioxide. Respiratory alkalosis is clearing too much of it, which is what hyperventilation from panic or altitude does. Metabolic acidosis is gaining acid or losing bicarbonate. Metabolic alkalosis is losing acid or gaining bicarbonate, prolonged vomiting being the classic route. Whichever system is not at fault compensates, which is why metabolic acidosis makes you breathe deep and fast.

None of this is self-diagnosable, and none of it is what happened during your set of squats. The acid shift in training is local, transient, and normal. Breathlessness out of proportion to the work, dizziness that does not clear, or chest tightness is a doctor conversation.

This is also where the buffering supplement category lives — sodium bicarbonate and beta-alanine. The mechanism behind both is real, which is more than most of the shelf can say. What the marketing loses is how narrow the window is: it applies to a specific band of effort duration, individual response varies widely, and the side effects are common enough to matter — gastrointestinal distress with bicarbonate, skin tingling with beta-alanine.
