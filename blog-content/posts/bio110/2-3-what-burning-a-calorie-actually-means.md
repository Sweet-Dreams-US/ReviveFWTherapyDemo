We say burning calories so often that the phrase has stopped meaning anything. But it is not a metaphor. The chemistry your cells run on a Tuesday afternoon is genuinely the same chemistry as a fire, with one crucial difference in how it is staged.

Understanding that difference explains why you breathe, why you get hot when you train, and why the number printed on a food label is an estimate rather than a fact about you.

## Four patterns cover most of what happens inside you

Chemists sort reactions into a few families, and your body is running all of them constantly.

Synthesis is building: linking glucose units into glycogen, stringing amino acids into muscle protein. Decomposition is the reverse, taking a large molecule apart into smaller ones — digestion in a sentence.

Displacement is swapping partners, which is how phosphate groups get shuffled between molecules to move energy around. Combustion is fuel plus oxygen yielding carbon dioxide, water, and energy released.

That last one is the family your metabolism belongs to. A campfire and a working muscle are running the same overall reaction.

## Burning means moving electrons

The deeper description is about electrons, not flames.

Oxidation is losing electrons. Reduction is gaining them. The two always happen together, because electrons have to go somewhere, and the pair is called a redox reaction.

Fuel molecules — fat and carbohydrate — are rich in electrons held in carbon-hydrogen bonds. Oxygen is unusually good at pulling electrons toward itself. Put them together and electrons move from fuel to oxygen, releasing energy in the process.

A fire does that in one uncontrolled rush. Your cells do the same net transfer in a long series of small steps, using carrier molecules — you may have seen them written as NAD and FAD — that pick up electrons from fuel and hand them down a chain built into your mitochondria. At the very end of that chain sits oxygen, waiting to accept the electrons and become water.

That is the actual reason you breathe. Oxygen is not a nutrient in any ordinary sense. It is the final electron acceptor, and without something to catch the electrons at the end, the whole chain backs up within seconds.

## Where the heat comes from

A reaction that releases energy to its surroundings is exothermic. Combustion is strongly exothermic, and the amount released is measurable as an enthalpy change.

Because your cells release that energy in steps, they can capture part of it in ATP, the molecule your body actually spends. The rest leaves as heat, and that portion is not a design failure. It is how you hold a body temperature near 98.6 degrees. In a Fort Wayne January, the heat coming off your metabolism is doing real work.

It also explains why you overheat during hard training. You are increasing the rate of an exothermic process inside an insulated body, and the heat has to leave through your skin and your breath faster than you are making it. That is a transport problem, not an energy problem.

## Where the number on the label came from

To measure the energy in food, chemists use a bomb calorimeter: a sealed steel chamber surrounded by water. The food is ignited and burned completely, and the temperature rise of the surrounding water tells you exactly how much energy was released.

Your gut is not a steel chamber. You do not absorb everything, some material passes through, and some energy leaves in urine. So the values on labels come from conversion factors — the familiar 4 per gram for carbohydrate and protein, 9 for fat — that were derived to account for typical human losses.

Those factors are averages, over a century old, and applied to every food in the same way. They are good enough for planning and too crude for precision.

Which brings up the honest limit. Thermodynamics is not negotiable, and nothing you can buy suspends it. There is no metabolism booster with meaningful evidence behind it, and the machine that says you burned 480 calories does not know your body. Where real leverage exists — more muscle, more daily movement, better sleep, consistent training — none of it is exciting enough to sell in a bottle.
