The cramp arrives in your calf at two in the morning, or in the last round of a conditioning piece, and the first thing anyone says is "you need electrolytes." Reasonable guess. It has also been a hard one for researchers to confirm, which is worth knowing before you buy another tub.

## What an electrolyte actually is

An electrolyte is a compound that dissolves into charged particles — ions — that carry electrical current. That is the entire definition, and the list that matters in a body is short: sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, magnesium.

Each has a personality. Sodium lives mostly outside your cells and largely sets the volume of the fluid out there; potassium is the inside counterpart; chloride generally follows sodium around.

Calcium is the odd one. Your cells spend energy continuously keeping its internal concentration near zero, precisely so a small release can mean something — signals are cheap when the background is quiet. Magnesium sits inside cells, acts as a cofactor for a long list of enzymes, and is the ion ATP actually pairs with.

## Your nerves and muscles literally run on a voltage

Separate charge across a barrier and you have a potential difference. A battery does it with electrodes and a chemical reaction; a cell does it with ion gradients across a membrane.

A pump called the sodium-potassium ATPase runs constantly, pushing sodium out and potassium in and burning ATP to do it. Combine those gradients with a membrane that lets some ions cross more easily than others and you get a resting membrane potential: the cell interior held negative relative to the outside, on the order of tens of millivolts.

An action potential is that voltage briefly flipping and traveling down the cell. Every heartbeat, every thought, every rep is that event repeated at speed.

Which is why the electrolyte story is so intuitive. The ions really are the electricity. The open question is whether an ordinary hard workout moves them enough to matter.

## The membrane that makes the voltage possible

None of that works without a barrier ions cannot cross, and the barrier assembles itself. Phospholipids are amphipathic — one end polar and comfortable in water, the other end two greasy fatty acid tails that are not. Drop them in water and they arrange themselves: tails tucked inward, polar heads facing out. Depending on the molecule's shape you get a micelle or a bilayer, a two-layer sheet.

That is the origin of every membrane in you. Nobody builds it — the hydrophobic effect does it for free, and the oily interior blocks charged ions from wandering across. No barrier, no gradient, no voltage, no contraction. The same chemistry explains soap, bile salts, and why fat-soluble vitamins need fat present to be absorbed.

## So does salt fix a cramp?

Here is the honest limit, and it is a big one. Exercise-associated muscle cramps have been studied for decades and the dehydration-and-depletion model has not held up. Cramps show up in people who are not meaningfully dehydrated. They tend to be local to the working muscle rather than body-wide, which is odd for a whole-body problem, and they often arrive late in an effort or with the muscle already shortened.

The leading alternative is altered neuromuscular control — the reflex loops regulating muscle tension drifting out of balance as fatigue accumulates. Also not proven. One suggestive clue: strongly flavored substances, pickle juice being the famous one, can stop a cramp faster than absorption could explain, which points at a reflex triggered in the mouth and throat rather than anything being replaced.

Fluid and sodium still matter for long, hot, sweaty work, and Fort Wayne in August is a genuine reason to salt your food and drink to thirst. That is separate from expecting sodium to abolish cramps.

On the supplement aisle, "chelated" means the mineral is held by an organic molecule gripping it at multiple points, from the Greek word for claw. Forms genuinely do differ in solubility and absorption — magnesium oxide is poorly soluble, glycinate and citrate less so. That much is established. What is not established, and gets sold hard, is that a better-absorbed form produces a better outcome in someone who was not deficient to begin with. Correcting a documented deficiency is a medical matter with a blood test behind it, and high-dose minerals compete with each other and interact with medications. Pharmacist or physician territory.

Cramps that are frequent, happen at rest, worsen, or come with weakness, numbness, or unusually dark urine belong in front of a doctor, not a supplement store.
