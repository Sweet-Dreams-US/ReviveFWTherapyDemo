The bottled water aisle now sells pH. Nine point five. Ionized. "Balances your body's pH." Before you decide whether that is worth three dollars a jug, it helps to know what pH actually measures — because the number on the bottle and the number in your blood have almost nothing to do with each other.

## What an acid actually is

Chemistry has three definitions of an acid, each broader than the last.

The oldest, from Arrhenius, is the simplest: an acid releases hydrogen ions in water, a base releases hydroxide ions. That works for hydrochloric acid and then stops working almost immediately.

The one worth carrying around is Brønsted–Lowry: an acid is a proton donor, a base is a proton acceptor. A hydrogen ion is just a proton — a hydrogen atom that has given up its electron — so acid-base chemistry is protons being handed from one molecule to another. Which means acid and base are not fixed identities. They are roles in a transaction, and water plays both depending on what it is standing next to.

Lewis went broadest: an acid accepts a pair of electrons, a base donates one — which is how a zinc ion parked inside an enzyme counts as an acid.

## Why the pH scale is stranger than it looks

Pure water is not inert. A small fraction constantly splits into hydrogen and hydroxide ions and recombines, and the product of those two concentrations is fixed at a given temperature. Push one up and the other must come down.

pH is a logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration, and that is the part people skip. Each whole number is a tenfold change. A solution at pH 5 has ten times the hydrogen ion concentration of pH 6, and a hundred times that of pH 7.

Anchor a few numbers. Your stomach runs around pH 1.5 to 3.5 — genuinely strong acid, produced deliberately, all day. Your blood sits between roughly 7.35 and 7.45, and that range is not a suggestion; move meaningfully outside it and you are in a hospital. Urine varies widely, on purpose, because it is one of the exits.

## Strong, weak, and why the difference matters

"Strong" does not mean concentrated. A strong acid dissociates essentially completely in water — hydrochloric acid hands over every proton it has. A weak acid only partly dissociates and settles into an equilibrium with most of itself still intact. How far it goes is captured by the acid dissociation constant.

Biology runs almost entirely on weak acids, and that is not an accident. A molecule that is only partly dissociated can absorb a proton when protons are plentiful and release one when they are scarce. It works as a shock absorber.

You can see this on a titration curve, the graph of pH as you add measured base to an acid. With a weak acid the curve holds a long flat stretch where you keep adding base and the pH barely moves, then jumps sharply once the acid runs out. That flat stretch is buffering, and your blood lives on one.

Neutralization is the reaction underneath all of it: acid plus base gives salt plus water. An antacid tablet is that reaction, run on purpose, in your stomach.

## So what happens to the alkaline water

It goes into a stomach sitting at pH 1.5 to 3.5. Neutralization happens, exactly as the chemistry predicts. Whatever survives is absorbed into a bloodstream defended by buffer systems, your lungs, and your kidneys — three overlapping mechanisms whose whole job is holding that 7.35 to 7.45 line.

Your urine pH does shift with what you eat and drink. That is real, and it is evidence the system is working rather than evidence you changed your blood.

Here is the honest limit, because this is not pure nonsense. Diet composition genuinely does change the acid load your kidneys handle, and there is legitimate ongoing research into dietary acid load and long-term bone and kidney outcomes, particularly in people whose kidney function is already reduced. What is not supported is the leap from that to a bottle of water raising blood pH or preventing disease. And if you have kidney disease, or take a medication whose absorption depends on stomach acid, that is a conversation with your physician, not with a label.

Worth saying plainly: the eating patterns marketed as alkaline are mostly vegetables and fruit. If the branding gets you eating more produce, you got a real benefit for the wrong reason.
