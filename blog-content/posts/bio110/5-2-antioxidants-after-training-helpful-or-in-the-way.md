You finish a session that took something out of you and reach for the tub with "antioxidant" on the front. The logic looks airtight: training makes free radicals, free radicals cause damage, antioxidants mop them up. The chemistry holds until the last step, and the last step is where it gets interesting.

## Six small handles that decide what a molecule does

Carbon skeletons are dull. The reactive groups hanging off them make one molecule sugar and another a hormone.

The hydroxyl group, an oxygen and hydrogen pair, is polar and makes things water-friendly. The carbonyl group, a carbon double-bonded to oxygen, is the reactive center sugars are built around. The carboxyl group is acidic and gives up a proton — every fatty acid and every amino acid carries one. The amino group is basic and accepts a proton, forming the other end of every amino acid.

The phosphate group carries strong negative charge and is the energy-transfer group in ATP as well as the backbone of DNA. The sulfhydryl group, a sulfur bonded to hydrogen, forms bridges that lock a protein into shape — what a perm does to hair, and the working end of glutathione, the antioxidant your cells make for themselves.

The practical takeaway: "natural versus synthetic" is usually the wrong question. The group and the geometry are the thing.

## Your body builds by removing water and takes apart by adding it

Two reactions account for most of what happens to the food you eat. Condensation joins two building blocks and releases a water molecule. Hydrolysis does the reverse, splitting a bond by inserting water back in.

Every polymer in you was assembled by the first and is dismantled by the second, constantly. You are less a structure than a construction site that never closes.

This settles a question people ask a lot. Collagen peptides get hydrolyzed in your digestive tract like any other protein, down to amino acids and small fragments. They do not travel intact to your knee. Some fragments do turn up in blood, and there is genuine ongoing research into whether they act as signals rather than raw material, but the evidence for joint and tendon outcomes is mixed and early. What is not chemically plausible is the version on the label, where eating connective tissue becomes connective tissue.

## The four families, briefly

Carbohydrates run from single sugars up to long storage and structural chains. Lipids are the odd family out, not true polymers, built from fatty acids and glycerol plus the sterols — membranes, hormones, dense energy storage. Proteins are amino acids joined by peptide bonds, which are condensation products, and do nearly every active job in a cell. Nucleic acids are chains of nucleotides: a sugar, a phosphate, a base.

Worth noticing: ATP is a nucleotide. Your energy currency is a chemical cousin of the letters in your DNA.

## What a free radical is, and why more is not automatically better

A free radical is a molecule with an unpaired electron. Electrons are stable in pairs, so an unpaired one makes a molecule aggressive — it grabs an electron from a neighbor, which turns the neighbor into a radical, which grabs from its neighbor. A chain reaction, not a single event.

Reactive oxygen species are the ones your body deals with most: superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, the hydroxyl radical. Your mitochondria produce them continuously as a byproduct of normal metabolism; your immune cells produce them deliberately as a weapon.

An antioxidant works by donating an electron and then being stable enough not to pass the problem along. Vitamin C and vitamin E do this. So do enzyme systems you build yourself — superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase — which handle most of the work.

Here is the turn. Reactive oxygen species produced during exercise are not only damage. They are part of the signal telling the cell to adapt: to build more mitochondria, to raise its own antioxidant defenses. Several trials giving high-dose vitamin C and E around training have found blunted adaptations rather than improved ones.

That result is not settled science. Doses, timing, populations, and outcome measures differ across studies and the findings are inconsistent. But the simple story — more antioxidants means faster recovery — does not survive contact with the data. Nobody can currently tell you an optimal antioxidant intake for a person who trains. Food-based intake and isolated high-dose supplements are also not the same thing, and the evidence separating them is thinner than either camp implies. A diagnosed deficiency is a different situation, and a medical one.
