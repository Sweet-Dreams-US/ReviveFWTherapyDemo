Walk down a supplement aisle and almost every label makes a version of the same promise. A handful of those products have real research behind them. Most do not, and the ones that do not are rarely lying about their ingredients — they are just selling a molecule that has nowhere to go once it is inside you.

The filter that separates the two is not marketing literacy. It is shape.

## A molecule is a shape, not an ingredient list

Atoms share electrons to form covalent bonds, and the pairs of electrons around an atom push each other as far apart as they can get. That repulsion sets the angles, and the angles set the shape.

Water is the classic case. Two hydrogens on an oxygen do not sit in a straight line — they bend to a little over 104 degrees, which leaves the molecule with a positive end and a negative end. That bend is why water dissolves salt at all.

Biology runs on that logic everywhere. Enzymes and receptors work by fit — a specific shape with a specific charge distribution meeting a matching pocket. A substance that contains the same elements but folds differently is a different substance as far as your cells are concerned. This is why "contains amino acids" tells you close to nothing.

## The charged particles your muscles actually run on

Some atoms do not share electrons, they hand them over. One atom takes, one gives, and the two opposite charges hold together as an ionic bond. Table salt is a rigid lattice of sodium and chloride built that way, which is why it is a hard crystal.

Drop it in water and the lattice comes apart into free ions, each wrapped in a shell of water molecules oriented by those bent, lopsided shapes. Those free ions are the working parts of your physiology: sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, phosphate.

Calcium deserves a specific mention. A contraction begins when calcium ions flood out of storage inside the muscle fiber and bind to a protein that lets the contraction machinery engage. Your muscle is not powered by calcium. It is switched by it, which is a different job and a much smaller quantity.

## What makes creatine different from the rest of the shelf

Creatine is a small molecule with a distribution of charge that your body already knows how to handle. There is a dedicated transporter for it in muscle membranes, and there is an existing job waiting for it.

That job is phosphate delivery. In the first seconds of a maximal effort, your cells regenerate ATP by pulling a phosphate off phosphocreatine and handing it over. More stored phosphocreatine means that pathway holds up slightly longer, which shows up as one or two more hard reps and, over months, more accumulated training.

Note what is not happening. Nothing was boosted or accelerated. A molecule was supplied to a system already built to use it, and it is among the most studied supplements in sport for exactly that reason.

Now the honest part. The short list with genuinely consistent support is short: creatine monohydrate, caffeine, protein powder treated as convenient food rather than magic, and vitamin D if you are actually low, worth asking about after an Indiana winter. Almost everything else on that wall is either untested in humans, tested at doses nobody takes, or supported by studies too small to mean much. Monohydrate is the form with the research; exotic salts and esters are sold on solubility claims, cost more, and show no real advantage. And if you take medication or have kidney issues, adding anything is a conversation with your doctor first.

## The weak forces that decide what mixes with what

Beyond full bonds, molecules tug on each other more gently, and those weak attractions run more of your biology than the strong ones.

Hydrogen bonding is the strongest of them: a hydrogen attached to oxygen or nitrogen reaching toward a nearby negative charge. Thousands of those hold a protein in its working shape. Heat them enough and they let go, which is what you are watching when an egg white turns solid — the protein has unfolded and cannot go back.

Fats have no charged ends, so they attract each other only through fleeting, weak dispersion forces. They stack together and refuse to mix with water, which is the whole reason vitamins A, D, E, and K are better absorbed alongside a meal containing some fat. Taking them with black coffee wastes part of the dose.

## The Revive Tip

Creatine only pays off if something in your week demands the energy system it supports. That means short, hard, heavy work: sets in the three-to-six-rep range on the competition racks, sled pushes on the turf, sprint repeats where you actually stop and recover between them.

If your training is all steady-state, you have supplied a system you rarely stress. Build two sessions a week around real intensity on the platforms and strength floor, then decide whether anything else in your cabinet has earned its place.
