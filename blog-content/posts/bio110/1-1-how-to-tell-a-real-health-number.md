Your scale says 184.6 pounds on Monday and 182.1 on Wednesday. The body fat percentage it hands you moved almost two points in a week, which would be a remarkable amount of tissue to lose. Your watch says you burned 612 calories in that class. The machine you were standing on said 480.

At least one of those numbers is wrong, and realistically all of them are off by some amount. The useful skill is not hunting for the true number. It is knowing what any given number is worth.

## Accurate and precise are two different things

A measurement is precise when it repeats. Step on the same scale five times in three minutes, get 184.6 every time, and that scale is precise. If your actual weight is 179, it is also wrong five times in a row.

A measurement is accurate when it lands close to the truth. Consumer devices are usually far more precise than they are accurate, and that is genuinely useful — a consistent error mostly cancels out when you compare this month to last month. It only helps if you hold the conditions still: same device, same time of day, similar hydration.

The decimal point is doing marketing work. In a lab, the digits you may report are limited by what the instrument can actually resolve — significant figures. A scale that can be off by three pounds has no business showing you a tenth of one.

## Why small errors turn into bigger ones

The body fat number on a home scale is not measured. It is calculated. Most of those devices send a small current through your body, measure the resistance, and feed that into an equation built from a population — along with your height, age, sex, and weight.

Every input carries its own error, and the final number inherits all of them at once. That stacking is called error propagation, and it is why the output can swing more than any single input did.

Resistance also depends heavily on body water, so a salty dinner or a sweaty session moves the reading without moving a gram of your actual tissue. Cardio equipment works the same way: it knows speed, incline, and the weight you typed in, and nothing about your body composition. The more steps between the raw physical measurement and the number on the screen, the more error rides along.

## A number without a unit is not information

Units are not bookkeeping. The whole system rests on a handful of base units — the kilogram, the meter, the second — and everything else is built from them. Tracking those units through a calculation is dimensional analysis, and it catches most real-world mistakes.

Concentration is where this bites. Blood sodium is reported in millimoles per liter, glucose in milligrams per deciliter, vitamin D in nanograms per milliliter. Same idea, three different scales, which is why two lab reports can look nothing alike while describing the same blood.

The mole is worth a minute. Chemists count particles instead of weighing grams, because reactions happen one particle at a time, and a mole is simply a fixed, very large count of them. A milliequivalent on a lab report is that same instinct applied to electrical charge, which is why electrolytes get reported that way.

The practical version: a milligram and a microgram differ by a factor of a thousand. On a label, that is the difference between a sensible dose and a serious mistake.

## More is not a straight line

Almost nothing in your body responds to a dose in a straight line. Plot response against dose and you typically get a curve that rises, flattens, and often bends back down. Too little iron is a problem. Too much iron is a different problem.

Lab values come from curves too. An instrument measures a signal — often how much light a sample absorbs — and converts it with a calibration curve built from known standards. The answer is solid in the middle of that curve and shakier at the edges.

The reference range printed next to your result is not a personal target either. It describes where most of a reference population sits.

Here is the honest limit. No consumer device tells you the truth about your body composition, and even clinical machines disagree between repeat scans on the same person on the same day. If a number actually matters medically, it belongs to a physician ordering a real test, not to an app.
