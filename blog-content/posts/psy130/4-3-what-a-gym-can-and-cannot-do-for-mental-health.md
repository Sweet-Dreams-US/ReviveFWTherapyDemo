The fitness industry has a line it repeats without much care: the gym is my therapy. It is usually said warmly and it contains something true. It is also, taken literally, wrong in a way that costs people years.

Training genuinely helps a lot of people feel better. It is not treatment for a mental health condition, and treating it as one is how someone spends four years grinding out sessions instead of spending eight weeks getting actual care that works.

Here is the honest version — what the evidence supports, where it is thinner than advertised, and who does what.

## These conditions are common, and they do not skip people who train

Anxiety disorders and depressive disorders are among the most common health conditions in adults. Being fit does not confer immunity, and there is a specific reason this gets missed in athletic and gym-going populations: the presentation often does not look like the stereotype.

Depression in someone who trains frequently does not show up as visible sadness. It shows up as flatness — things that used to matter stop mattering. Training that used to be the good part of the day becomes another obligation. Sleep changes in either direction. Appetite changes. Concentration goes. There is often irritability rather than tearfulness, and a persistent, disproportionate sense of being a failure or a burden.

Anxiety often shows up first in the body: chest tightness, stomach trouble, jaw and neck tension, a heart rate that will not settle. Then the mental piece, which is the inability to stop scanning for what is going to go wrong.

The rough threshold used in screening is a change from your own baseline, most days, for at least two weeks, that is affecting how you function. That is a prompt to talk to someone, not a diagnosis. Diagnosis is a licensed clinician's job, and it is genuinely more complicated than a checklist — thyroid problems, sleep apnea, anemia, medication effects, and grief can all present similarly, which is exactly why the assessment matters.

## What the exercise-and-depression evidence actually says

This is worth getting precise about, because it is oversold in one direction and dismissed in the other.

Exercise is studied primarily as an **adjunctive** intervention — something added alongside standard care rather than replacing it. Across a large body of randomized trials, structured exercise programs are associated with reductions in depressive symptoms, and the finding is consistent enough to be taken seriously. Major clinical guidelines now include physical activity as a supportive component of care.

Now the problems with that literature, which are real.

Most individual trials are small. You cannot blind participants to whether they are exercising, and you often cannot blind the assessors either, which inflates results. Comparison conditions vary wildly, and some are close to no-treatment. There is documented publication bias. And effect sizes tend to shrink — sometimes considerably — when analyses are restricted to the highest-quality trials with active control groups.

So the honest claim is: exercise appears to help with depressive symptoms, the effect is probably real, and it is probably smaller than the headlines suggest.

On dose: the studied range is broadly what public health guidelines already recommend — moderate aerobic work, resistance training, or a mix, several days a week. More is not better here. This is precisely the territory where a helpful habit turns into a compulsive one, and grinding harder when you feel worse is not a treatment protocol.

The hard lines, stated plainly:

Exercise does not replace therapy. Exercise does not replace medication. Nobody at a gym — not a coach, not a trainer, not the strongest person in the room — should be advising you about your prescriptions. If you are in treatment and it is working, keep going, and bring exercise to your prescriber as an addition rather than a swap.

## When it is more urgent than that

Some situations are not "make an appointment next month" situations.

Signals worth taking seriously, in yourself or someone else: talking about being a burden or about others being better off, hopelessness that does not lift, giving away possessions or putting affairs in order, a sudden unexplained calm after a long bad stretch, a sharp increase in drinking or substance use, and withdrawing from everyone.

If it is you, or someone you are with: **call or text 988** to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the United States. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

Two things that are worth knowing and that most people do not know.

First, asking directly does not plant the idea. This is one of the most consistently supported findings in suicide prevention research, and the fear of it keeps people vague when directness would help. You can ask: "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Plainly, without flinching, without euphemism.

Second, what helps is unglamorous. Stay with them. Listen without rushing to fix. Do not promise to keep it secret. Help them make the call rather than telling them to make it. If there is access to means — firearms, stockpiled medication — reducing that access matters, and it is a conversation worth having directly with the person or their family.

## Who is qualified to do what

This is the boundary that protects you, and it gets blurry in gyms constantly.

**Coaching** covers training design, behavior and habit structure, accountability, and non-clinical support. A good coach can ask how you are doing, can notice when something has changed, can adjust a program around a hard stretch, and can refer you to someone.

**Counseling and psychotherapy** are delivered by licensed clinicians — psychologists, licensed clinical social workers, licensed mental health counselors, licensed marriage and family therapists. They assess, diagnose, and treat mental health conditions. Licensure means supervised clinical hours, an exam, a board, and consequences for harm.

**Medical care** is physicians, including psychiatrists, and other prescribers. Diagnosis, medication, and the differential workup that rules out physical causes.

**Medical nutrition therapy** is a registered dietitian's scope, not a coach's.

The line for anyone working on a gym floor is clear enough. A coach can notice, ask, listen, and refer. A coach cannot diagnose, cannot treat, cannot provide counseling, and should not be walking anyone through trauma between sets. Somebody willing to do all of that anyway is not more caring. They are working past their training with no supervision and no accountability, and you are the one carrying the risk.

A good referral is not a rejection. It sounds like: "This is past what I do, and I would rather you talk to someone who does this well. Here is who I would call."
