You got through a brutal February. Then November arrived with the same hours, the same commute, the same kids, the same inbox — and it flattened you. You were short with people you like. You skipped sessions you would normally never skip.

Nothing about the week changed. Something about you did.

That gap is the most useful idea in the entire psychology of stress, because it points directly at the part you can do something about.

## Stress is not the event. It is the math you run on the event.

The dominant framework here is the transactional model, developed by Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman. It says stress is not a property of the situation. It is what happens between the situation and you.

You run two quick calculations, mostly without noticing.

The first is **primary appraisal**: is this a threat to something I care about? A deadline at a job you have already mentally quit is not the same event as a deadline at a job you are trying to keep.

The second is **secondary appraisal**: do I have what it takes to handle this? Skills, time, sleep, money, people, past experience with this exact kind of problem.

Stress shows up when the demand outruns your perceived resources. That word "perceived" is doing real work. It is not a measurement, it is a read — and reads can be wrong in both directions.

This is not the same as saying stress is all in your head. The deadline is real. The diagnosis is real. Winter in Fort Wayne is genuinely dark and genuinely long. But your read of your own resources is a second variable in the equation, and unlike the deadline, it is one you can move.

It also explains November. Same demand, thinner resource column: sleep debt from the time change, six weeks of missed training, one fewer person you were actually talking to.

## What the first ninety seconds look like inside you

Two systems fire, at two different speeds.

The fast one is the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary pathway. Your sympathetic nervous system signals the adrenal medulla — the core of the gland sitting on top of each kidney — and adrenaline and noradrenaline are in your bloodstream within seconds. Heart rate up, blood pressure up, airways open, blood shunted toward skeletal muscle, digestion dropped to the bottom of the priority list.

The slower one is the **HPA axis**, which stands for hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal. Your hypothalamus releases a signaling hormone, your pituitary answers with another, and your adrenal cortex releases cortisol. That takes minutes to peak and considerably longer to clear.

Cortisol frees up glucose, keeps blood vessels responsive, and quiets down systems you do not need in the next ten minutes. It is not a toxin. It follows a daily rhythm, highest shortly after you wake up, tapering across the day. You would be in serious trouble without it.

Here is the part most stress content leaves out: this is the same machinery that makes hard training work. A heavy set of squats is a stressor. A 30-yard sprint is a stressor. The acute stress response has a beginning, a middle, and — critically — an end. The response is not the problem. The failure to end is the problem.

### Why "lower your cortisol" is a bad goal

Cortisol has become the villain in a lot of supplement marketing and a lot of short-form video, and the story being sold does not match the physiology.

Nobody can read your cortisol off your face. A single saliva or blood sample tells you very little, because the value swings across the day and responds to your commute, your coffee, and the act of getting your blood drawn. "Adrenal fatigue" — the idea that chronic stress wears your adrenal glands out — is not a recognized diagnosis, and major endocrinology bodies have said clearly that the evidence does not support it as a condition.

If you feel exhausted in a way that does not resolve, that deserves an actual workup with an actual physician. It does not deserve a bottle of adaptogens off a shelf.

## What changes when the response never fully switches off

Chronic stress is not a bigger version of acute stress. It is a different problem: a system built for sprints being asked to idle high for months.

The research links it to health outcomes through three main pathways.

**Cardiovascular.** Repeated pressure surges, exaggerated reactivity to small provocations, and slower return to baseline afterward. Over years, that pattern is associated with cardiovascular risk.

**Immune.** Sustained stress signaling shifts immune function rather than simply suppressing it. It is associated with slower wound healing, more frequent minor infections, and higher circulating inflammatory markers.

**Metabolic.** Appetite and food choices shift, sleep degrades, and glucose handling changes. The sleep loss then feeds straight back into the stress response, which is what makes this self-sustaining.

Now the honest part. Nearly all of that evidence is population-level and correlational. Stressed people also tend to sleep less, drink more, move less, and have less money and less access to care — and untangling those threads is genuinely hard. No one can look at you and tell you that your stress caused a specific condition, and anyone who does is guessing with confidence.

What the evidence supports is a direction, not a verdict: sustained, unresolved stress load is associated with worse health outcomes across a lot of systems. That is enough to act on. It is not enough to diagnose anybody.

## How to tell whether you are carrying more than you think

Researchers measure this three ways, and each has a lesson buried in it.

The **Perceived Stress Scale**, developed by Sheldon Cohen, asks how unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded the last month has felt. Notice that it measures your appraisal on purpose — because appraisal is what predicts outcomes.

**Life event inventories** count major events and assign each a fixed weight: a move, a divorce, a death, a new job. They are useful and also criticized, because identical events land completely differently depending on who they land on.

**Daily hassles** research found something that surprised people: accumulated small friction — the commute, the unanswered email, the dishwasher, the thing with the insurance — tracks health outcomes at least as well as the big dramatic events do. Your life is probably not being wrecked by one catastrophe. It is being sanded down by forty small things.

For your own use, you do not need an instrument. Put a 0 to 10 number in your training log every day, next to hours of sleep. Two weeks gives you a baseline. Watch for drift, not single bad days.

And watch the body-side signals that show up before you consciously register the load: resting heart rate creeping up, warmups taking longer to feel normal, falling asleep fine but waking at three, appetite strange in either direction, a short fuse with people you actually like, and quiet avoidance of sessions you used to look forward to.

Once you can see the load, you have two levers. You can work the resource side — sleep, support, skill, competence, having a plan. Or you can work the appraisal side, which is not about thinking positive. It is about thinking accurately.
