You meant it. When you signed up, you were not lying to yourself or anyone else — you genuinely intended to train three days a week, and you still intend to. The intention is sitting right there, fully intact, six weeks after the last time you went.

Researchers have a name for this: the intention–behavior gap. It is one of the most reliably documented findings in health psychology, and it is the reason "just decide to commit" is such useless advice. Deciding was never the hard part.

Four models dominate this field. They are not equally good, and one of the most popular is the weakest.

## The scare-yourself-healthy model, and why the scare wears off

The **Health Belief Model** is the oldest of the four. It came out of US Public Health Service work in the 1950s, trying to understand why people would not show up for free tuberculosis screening. It says your likelihood of acting depends on five things:

- **Perceived susceptibility** — do you believe this could happen to you
- **Perceived severity** — do you believe it would be bad
- **Perceived benefits** — do you believe the action would help
- **Perceived barriers** — what it costs you in time, money, effort, discomfort
- **Cues to action** — the trigger that starts things, like a physical, a scare, a friend's heart attack

Most health marketing leans hard on the first two. Get people frightened enough and they will act. In practice, fear does produce a spike in behavior, and that spike is small and short-lived. Everyone at a gym knows what the first two weeks of January look like, and everyone knows what the second week of February looks like.

The interesting finding is which component actually carries the weight. Across decades of reviews of the model, **perceived barriers** has been the most consistent predictor of whether people follow through — stronger than susceptibility or severity. What stops people is not that they underestimate the risk. It is that the action is inconvenient, expensive, uncomfortable, or socially awkward.

That reframes the whole problem. You do not need a scarier reason. You need a cheaper action.

## Your intentions are real, and they do not reach Tuesday

The **Theory of Planned Behavior** refined this. It says behavior flows from intention, and intention is built from three inputs: your attitude toward the behavior, your subjective norms (what you believe people around you think and do), and your perceived behavioral control (whether you believe you can actually pull it off).

It is a good model of intention. That is also its problem. Intention turns out to be a much weaker bridge to behavior than the model implies. A large share of people who form a genuine, sincere intention to exercise never act on it — researchers sometimes call them "inclined abstainers," which is an uncomfortably accurate description of most of us.

So the practical question is not how to want it more. It is what sits between intention and action. Mostly: specificity of plan, friction in the environment, competing demands, and whether anything cues the behavior at the moment it needs to happen.

If you have read the previous post in this series, this is where if-then planning earns its keep. Intentions that specify a situation get acted on far more often than intentions that specify only an outcome.

## "Stages of change" is useful language and weak science

The **Transtheoretical Model** is the famous one — precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance. You have almost certainly encountered it, probably in a workplace wellness program or a corporate training deck.

Its genuine contribution was to stop treating everyone the same. Someone who has not considered changing needs a different conversation than someone who has a gym bag in the car. That insight is real and it made a lot of practitioners better at their jobs.

**The honest limit, and it is a big one:** the model has not held up well under scrutiny. The stage boundaries are defined by arbitrary time cut-points — the line between "preparing" and "acting" is drawn at a number someone picked. Behavior change often does not proceed in discrete stages at all; it is messier and more continuous than the staircase implies. Most damaging, stage-matched interventions, where the program is tailored to the person's stage, have not consistently outperformed well-designed interventions that ignore stages entirely. In 2005, a prominent addiction researcher published an editorial in a major journal explicitly calling for the model to be retired, and the debate has never really been settled in its favor.

Use the vocabulary if it helps you talk about where someone is. Do not build your program around the staircase, and be skeptical of anyone selling a system on the strength of it.

## A better question: which of the three is actually missing

The most useful of the four is also the most recent and the least famous. **COM-B**, developed by Susan Michie and colleagues as the hub of the Behaviour Change Wheel, says that any behavior requires three things simultaneously:

**Capability** — can you do it. This splits into physical capability (mobility, strength, pain) and psychological capability (do you know what to do, can you follow the program, do you have the attention for it).

**Opportunity** — does your world allow it. Physical opportunity is time, money, equipment, access, distance. Social opportunity is whether the people around you make it normal or make it weird.

**Motivation** — do you want to. This splits into reflective motivation (your considered goals and beliefs) and automatic motivation (habits, impulses, emotional responses in the moment).

All three have to be present. Missing any one produces no behavior — which means the same failure, from the outside, can have three completely different causes, and the fixes do not transfer.

This is why generic advice fails so consistently. If your problem is capability, more motivation is useless. If your problem is opportunity — you work rotating shifts and the gym you joined closes early — a mindset overhaul will not help. And if you genuinely have both capability and opportunity and still do not go, then and only then is motivation the thing to work on.

Before your next attempt, name the one that actually broke last time. Be honest, and be specific. "I don't know what to do once I'm there" is a capability problem with a straightforward fix. "I can't afford the plan I signed up for" is an opportunity problem, and shame will not solve it.

One caveat worth naming: sometimes what looks like a motivation failure is a clinical issue — persistent pain, untreated sleep apnea, depression, an anxiety problem that makes crowded rooms genuinely hard. Those are real barriers with real professional treatment, and a licensed clinician is the right person for them. Sorting that out first is not an excuse. It is the actual fix.

### What all four models share, and where they fall short

Every one of these is better at predicting behavior than at producing it. A model can identify what correlates with change without telling you what to do on Monday. That gap between prediction and intervention is the honest state of this field, and it is why the next post in this series is about specific techniques rather than more theory.
