Gym culture is very good at praising the thing it should sometimes be worried about. Training through an injury gets called dedication. Never missing a session gets called discipline. Eating the same four foods gets called being locked in.

Most of the time, those are just descriptions of a committed person. Sometimes they are describing something that has quietly stopped serving the person doing it, and the environment is the last place to notice, because the environment is applauding.

This post is about how to tell the difference — for yourself, or for someone you train next to. It is not a diagnostic tool, and it is not a substitute for a professional. It is a set of signals worth taking seriously.

## Where the picture in your head of your own body comes from

Body image is not one thing. Researchers break it into four components: how you **perceive** your body's size and shape, how you **feel** about it, what you **think** and believe about it, and what you **do** as a result — checking, avoiding, covering, comparing.

The mechanism that turns a cultural ideal into personal distress is **internalization**. Everyone is aware of the ideal. It only starts doing damage when you adopt it as the standard you are supposed to meet. Awareness is unavoidable. Internalization is the variable.

Social media gets blamed for all of this, and the evidence is more specific than the headlines. Most of the research is correlational, and where experiments exist they mostly show short-term effects on mood and body satisfaction after appearance-focused exposure. The more consistent finding is not about screen time in general but about **appearance comparison** specifically — looking at bodies and measuring yourself against them.

That distinction is the honest version, and it also matters practically. Deleting the app helps some people. It does not help nearly as much as expected in others, because the comparison habit is portable. It follows you into the gym, onto the pool deck, and into a photo from four years ago.

## Disordered eating is more common than the diagnoses, and both matter

There is a clinical category and there is a much larger territory around it.

The DSM-5-TR recognizes diagnoses including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, and a category for other specified feeding and eating disorders. Validated screening instruments exist — the SCOFF, the EAT-26, the EDE-Q are the common ones — and it is worth understanding what they are. They are **screens**. They flag people who should have a conversation with a clinician. They do not diagnose anyone, and no article, quiz, or coach can.

Outside those diagnoses sits a much larger group of people with disordered patterns: rigid food rules, guilt after eating, chronic restriction, compensating for meals with training, cycles of overly strict weeks and collapsed weekends. These patterns are common, they cost people a lot, and they do not require a diagnosis to be worth addressing.

A few facts that get in the way when people do not know them. Eating disorders occur across every body size, every gender, and every age. They are not visible. Weight is not the indicator — someone in a completely unremarkable body can be seriously unwell, and this is one of the main reasons people go years without help.

If any of this is landing while you read it, the right person is a licensed professional: a physician, a therapist, or a registered dietitian with specific eating disorder training. Not a coach, not a training partner, and not this article. That is not a brush-off. It is that this is a real clinical area with real treatment that works, and the people who do it are trained for exactly this.

## The signals a gym is actually positioned to notice

Exercise dependence and compulsive exercise are the patterns most likely to show up on a gym floor, and they are the ones most likely to be mistaken for virtue.

The signals researchers describe include:

- Training through injury or illness, and getting defensive when someone points it out
- Distress, guilt, or anxiety when a session is missed, out of proportion to what was missed
- Sessions that are non-negotiable in a way that costs relationships, sleep, or work
- Escalating volume to get the same relief that less used to give
- Using training to compensate for eating, or earning food with training
- Rigidity — the plan cannot flex for a sick kid, a work trip, or a bad night's sleep
- The training has stopped being **for** anything; it is now just something that has to be done

Here is the useful contrast. A committed athlete's training serves a goal, and it flexes when the goal requires it — they will take the deload, skip the session before a competition, and sleep in when sleep is the higher-value input. Dependence is rigid, and it defends itself.

The other tell is what happens on a forced rest week. Some people find it annoying. Some people find it genuinely unbearable. That difference is worth paying attention to.

If you are reading that list and recognizing yourself, the move is not "take a week off and see." It is to say it out loud to someone qualified. Compulsive exercise frequently travels with anxiety, depression, or disordered eating, and those are treatable.

## Muscle dysmorphia and the part of this men rarely say out loud

**Muscle dysmorphia** is recognized as a specifier within body dysmorphic disorder. It involves preoccupation with the idea that one's body is insufficiently muscular or insufficiently lean — very often in people who are, by any outside measure, notably muscular.

It looks like repeated mirror checking, or avoiding mirrors entirely. Wearing baggy clothing to hide a body someone has spent years building. Turning down plans that interfere with training or with prepared food. Rigid eating. A great deal of time lost to thinking about it.

It is under-recognized partly because the behaviors it produces are the exact behaviors the culture rewards.

Adjacent to this is the use of appearance- and performance-enhancing drugs. Some plain facts, without moralizing.

Anabolic-androgenic steroids carry documented cardiovascular, hepatic, endocrine, and psychiatric effects, and the risk is dose- and duration-related. SARMs are not approved by the FDA for human use, and the agency has issued public warnings about products sold as SARMs — including that testing has repeatedly found what is in the bottle is not what is on the label. And under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, supplements in the United States are not reviewed for safety or effectiveness before they go on sale.

If you are using or seriously considering it, the person to talk to is a physician who will have an actual conversation rather than a lecture. Those physicians exist. Going in honestly gets you monitoring — bloodwork, blood pressure, lipids, hormones — which is the single biggest difference between informed risk and blind risk.

## The Revive Tip

If the mirror is the thing driving your training, change what you measure. Move a block of your week onto the performance turf: sled pushes, sprint work down the 30-yard strip, a conditioning loop you time. Numbers that go up whether or not you like what you see in a photograph.

That is not a trick to feel better about your body. It is a different scoreboard, and for a lot of people it is a far more sustainable one.
